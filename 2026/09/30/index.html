★★★ 1. JWST lensed quasar dark matter survey V: Hints of self-interacting dark matter from 29 quadruply imaged quasars

D. Gilman et al.
arXiv:2609.35974

The authors use flux-ratio perturbations in 29 quadruply imaged quasars to infer the abundance of core-collapsed dark-matter halos and subhalos between 106 and 1010.7M⊙. Such deeply collapsed objects are a characteristic late-time outcome of some velocity-dependent self-interacting-dark-matter models.

After marginalizing over globular clusters, subhalo abundance, lens macromodels and collapsed-halo structure, the data prefer models containing core-collapsed halos. The reported Bayes factors disfavor collisionless CDM relative to the tested SIDM populations by between 6:1 and 24:1. A CDM-only fit requires both more globular clusters than expected and a subhalo abundance above standard N-body predictions.

History

Earlier simulations showed that quasar flux ratios should be unusually sensitive to SIDM core collapse, forecasting roughly 20:1 odds against CDM for a favorable 50-lens sample. (SIDM lensing forecasts) The JWST survey has already used essentially the same sample to set competitive warm-dark-matter limits—mWDM≳5.6−6.4 keV depending on the substructure prior—but those analyses tested suppression of halo abundance, not the enhanced central densities generated by collapse. (JWST warm-dark-matter analysis)

This is therefore the first substantial population-level observational hint for SIDM core collapse from strong lenses, rather than another forecast. It is not yet evidence for a unique particle interaction: the odds vary by a factor of four under plausible assumptions about globular clusters and SIDM subhalos, and the inference depends on extrapolating collapsed-halo profiles below scales directly resolved by simulations. Independent lens samples and the promised particle-physics interpretation are essential before treating this as a challenge to CDM rather than a lens-population modeling tension.

★★★ 2. Constraining dark matter using 20-year INTEGRAL/IBIS observations II: Sub-GeV dark matter

Jordan Koechler, Pedro De la Torre Luque
arXiv:2609.35968

Twenty years of diffuse INTEGRAL/IBIS hard-X-ray data are analyzed jointly in energy and Galactic longitude. The background model combines inverse-Compton emission from cosmic-ray electrons with unresolved accreting white dwarfs; secondary inverse-Compton photons from dark-matter-produced electrons and positrons are propagated consistently.

For 1 MeV–5 GeV dark matter annihilating or decaying to e+e−, μ+μ−, or π+π−, the analysis obtains limits of approximately

10−28≲⟨σv⟩/(cm3s−1)≲10−26,τ≳1025 s

at masses of a few MeV, with improvements reaching two orders of magnitude over previous X-ray bounds. The results are also translated into dark-photon and scalar-Higgs portal parameters.

History

The foundational INTEGRAL analysis already showed that secondary inverse-Compton emission greatly strengthens limits above roughly 20 MeV. (Earlier INTEGRAL analysis) A subsequent combination of INTEGRAL, XMM-Newton, NuSTAR and Suzaku produced leading bounds over parts of the 0.1−1 GeV range. (Combined X-ray constraints) The present gain comes from twenty years of IBIS exposure and, more importantly, fitting spectral and longitudinal morphology simultaneously instead of treating the diffuse intensity as a one-dimensional bound.

The improvement is substantial, but not purely exposure-driven or detector-systematics-limited. It relies on the adopted cosmic-ray propagation model, unresolved-white-dwarf template, Galactic radiation fields and dark-matter density profile. These astrophysical nuisance choices should be compared directly with the earlier multi-instrument analysis before the strongest limits are used in global portal-model fits.

★★★ 3. Combined constraints on the diffuse flux of cosmic neutrinos between 1016 and 1026 eV

S. V. Troitsky
arXiv:2609.36632

Published searches from optical Cherenkov telescopes, air-shower arrays, in-ice and balloon radio detectors, and lunar radio experiments use mutually incompatible flavor, binning and confidence-limit conventions. Troitsky reconstructs their energy-dependent exposures, converts them to a common all-flavor ν+ν¯ convention, and combines event counts and backgrounds in a Poisson likelihood.

The output is a reproducible set of 90%-confidence, one-decade quasi-differential limits over ten orders of magnitude in energy. Model spectra—including cosmogenic neutrinos and selected exotic scenarios—can be folded directly through the combined exposure; machine-readable exposure and limit tables are supplied.

History

IceCube and Auger previously published strong quasi-differential constraints, but as experiment-specific results with different energy intervals and statistical definitions. (IceCube limits; Auger limits) Broader “unified neutrino spectrum” reviews compiled fluxes through the PeV scale rather than constructing a likelihood combination of ultrahigh-energy null searches. (Grand Unified Neutrino Spectrum review)

The advance is consequently methodological and highly useful, not a new neutrino event or detector result. It appears to be the first reproducible exposure-level combination spanning the full 1016−1026 eV range. It is nevertheless an external reconstruction from published summaries, not a collaboration-sanctioned joint likelihood; incomplete covariance information, changing cross-section assumptions and detector-specific systematics cannot all be propagated exactly.

★★★ 4. Discovery of the Infrared Counterpart of the Rapid Burster with JWST

Malina M. Desai, Kevin B. Burdge, Cristina Pallanca, Saul Rappaport, Francesco R. Ferraro, Barbara Lanzoni, Mason Ng, Paul Draghis, Emma T. Chickles, Ilaria Caiazzo, Deepto Chakrabarty
arXiv:2609.35956

JWST/NIRCam observations obtained while MXB 1730−335 was X-ray active identify an unambiguous infrared counterpart to the Rapid Burster—the only known neutron star producing both thermonuclear Type-I and accretion-driven Type-II bursts.

The source fades by approximately 2.5 magnitudes in F200W and shows strong variability on minute timescales. The identification provides a practical target for infrared spectroscopy and coordinated X-ray/IR observations of the donor and accretion flow.

History

Chandra, HST and ground-based infrared imaging had narrowed the field but could not isolate a uniquely variable object in Liller 1’s extremely crowded and extinguished core. (Earlier counterpart search) A 2025 HST/Gemini study proposed a variable optical candidate, explicitly leaving its association unconfirmed. (2025 optical candidate) JWST’s active-state variability converts that candidate-level situation into a compelling identification.

The result is an observational discovery rather than yet a determination of the binary parameters. The donor’s spectral type, orbital period and the relation between infrared variability and individual Type-II bursts remain unmeasured.

★★★ 5. Joint Mass-Radius and Magnetic-Geometry Inference of PSR J0740+6620

Chun Huang, Tuomo Salmi, Alexander Y. Chen
arXiv:2609.37928

Instead of fitting phenomenological emitting spots, the authors connect the NICER/XMM pulse profile of the massive pulsar J0740+6620 to two low-dimensional magnetospheric geometries: a centered dipole–quadrupole and a shifted dipole.

The shifted dipole is favored by

ΔlnZ=5.65,

corresponding to a Bayes factor near 285 under the chosen priors. It gives

M=2.059−0.069+0.068M⊙,R=13.28−1.07+1.47 km.

Both tested configurations require an X-ray footprint considerably smaller than a standard force-free polar cap; exploratory photon-conversion calculations do not reproduce that compactness.

History

The two independent 2024 NICER analyses obtained R=12.49−0.88+1.28 km and 12.92−1.13+2.09 km using flexible hotspot parameterizations. (Salmi et al. (2024); Dittmann et al. (2024)) The new radius is fully consistent with both, so this does not materially alter neutron-star equation-of-state constraints. The important result is that a restricted, magnetically motivated hotspot model preserves the radius inference while making a testable statement about polar-cap heating.

Only two idealized field families were compared, and the preferred model itself fails to explain the required footprint through the simple heating prescription. The large Bayes factor should therefore be read as preference within this narrow model space, not as a definitive magnetic-field reconstruction.

★★☆ 6. First Measurement of the Superscaling Variable as a Function of Visible Kinematics in Charged-Current Quasi-Elastic Neutrino–Nucleus Scattering

MINERvA Collaboration
arXiv:2609.37335

MINERvA measures a visible-kinematics version of the quasielastic superscaling variable, ψvis′, in νμ-carbon CCQE-like events. Because its peak is externally calibrated, shifts directly expose nuclear missing energy that would bias reconstructed neutrino energy.

Five nuclear-interaction models are compared through forward folding. Their predictions diverge most strongly—and generally fit least well—at low proton recoil energy and low muon transverse momentum. The relativistic mean-field model gives the best peak position among those tested.

History

Superscaling and RMF/SuSAv2 models were developed using electron scattering and had already been confronted with inclusive neutrino cross sections. (SuSAv2 model) The new contribution is an experimentally unfolded, visible final-state variable designed specifically to diagnose missing-energy biases rather than another inclusive-rate comparison.

That makes it directly useful for oscillation-energy systematics, although extrapolation from carbon and MINERvA’s few-GeV flux to argon experiments still requires generator-level validation. The conclusion that RMF performs best is restricted to the measured projections and the five tested implementations.

★★☆ 7. Search for dark matter annihilation in the Sun using the full ANTARES dataset

ANTARES Collaboration
arXiv:2609.37422

The final 2007–2022 ANTARES exposure is searched for neutrinos from WIMP annihilation in the Sun. No excess is observed. Spin-dependent and spin-independent WIMP–proton limits are reported for masses from 35 GeV to 10 TeV, assuming 100% branching to bb¯, τ+τ−, or W+W−.

The limits improve previous ANTARES results by factors of roughly two to three.

History

The earlier analysis used only 2007–2012 data and covered 50 GeV–5 TeV. (Earlier ANTARES analysis) The expanded mass range and factor-of-few sensitivity gain make this the definitive ANTARES solar-WIMP result, but it is an exposure-driven legacy improvement rather than a qualitatively new search technique or globally new WIMP regime.

Translation from a neutrino-flux limit to a scattering cross section assumes capture–annihilation equilibrium in the Sun, a Galactic velocity distribution and single-channel annihilation. Those assumptions can fail in models with suppressed annihilation or mixed final states.

★★☆ 8. Trace isotopes set the neutron horizon of liquid argon

Yasser Maghrbi
arXiv:2609.37087

Near 57 keV, destructive nuclear interference makes 40Ar nearly transparent to neutrons. The 0.4% natural abundance of 36Ar and 38Ar consequently supplies more than 90% of the total opacity, amplifying their effect by a factor of about 230.

Published transmission data exclude the 36Ar evaluation used by default in FLUKA and instead constrain its cross section to approximately 2−11 b. In a DUNE-scale simulation, adopting data-consistent evaluations shifts the fiducial fraction of 41Ar calibration captures by 9–13%, although the total yield changes by only 2%.

History

Neutron-transport studies normally treat natural argon as effectively 40Ar; no earlier work appears to have isolated this trace-isotope dominance in the interference window and propagated it to DUNE calibration geometry. The result is novel and potentially operationally important, but rests on existing transmission datasets rather than a new dedicated isotope measurement. A modern 36Ar measurement around 57 keV is the clean way to resolve the discrepancy among nuclear-data libraries.

The 30 September selection includes no new UHECR spectrum, composition or anisotropy measurement and no new precision oscillation-parameter result.

Verified science news

Starship reaches orbit for the first time, but its demonstration flight ends early

On 28 September 2026, SpaceX’s Flight 14 placed Starship into orbit for the first time. This is a major propulsion and launch-system milestone for a vehicle central to NASA’s planned lunar architecture.

The intended ten-hour, six-orbit demonstration ended early after one of Starship’s six engines shut down prematurely. The achievement therefore establishes orbital capability, but not yet the long-duration reliability, restart and recovery performance required for operational lunar missions. (SpaceX mission page; Flight report)

ESA’s Juice completes its September 2026 Earth gravity assist

ESA confirmed that the Jupiter Icy Moons Explorer passed 8,640 km above Earth on 28 September, changing direction by approximately 20∘ and gaining about 3.5 kms−1. Its ten science instruments also used Earth and the Moon as calibration targets.

The maneuver places Juice on course for one final Earth encounter in January 2029 and arrival at Jupiter in July 2031. It was a planned navigation milestone, not a new planetary-science discovery. (ESA announcement)
