ArXiv Highlights

1. Axion cavities: a correction to the observable, not a new exclusion

J. F. Bourhill, E. C. I. Paterson, M. Goryachev and M. E. Tobar — “Searching for Ultra-Light Axions with Twisted Cavity Resonators of Anyon Rotational Symmetry with Bulk Modes of Non-Zero Helicity.” arXiv:2208.01640v4. First submitted 1 August 2022; v4 submitted 13 October 2026, 23:33 UTC, appearing in the 15 October listing. Original article: Physical Review D 108, 052014 (2023); the authors say an erratum has been submitted.

The corrected Maxwell/sideband calculation retains both Fourier components of the real axion field. For the complex overlap C=∫E·B*dV, its real part drives amplitude modulation; its imaginary, helicity part drives phase modulation. The proposed twisted cavities therefore require phase-sensitive readout.

The 10−22–10−14 eV target is prospective. Sensitivity curves assume a −160 dBc/Hz residual phase-noise floor, local axion density 0.45 GeV/cm³, and signal-to-noise ratio 1. These are benchmarks, not confidence limits. Room-temperature cavity tests validate resonances; they do not demonstrate the projected dark-matter reach. Full corrected analysis, Sections III–IV.

History

Axion–photon coupling converts an oscillating dark-matter field into an electromagnetic response. Established searches must calibrate both that response and its noise. For example, ABRACADABRA-10 cm used a toroidal magnet, pickup circuit and SQUID, magnet-off vetoes, and a profiled noise model to obtain observed one-sided 95% coupling limits in the 0.41–8.27 neV range.

That mass interval differs from this proposal's target. A projected phase-noise benchmark cannot be ranked against observed laboratory or astrophysical exclusions as an achieved improvement. The substantive correction is which quadrature carries the signal: unchanged numerical curves remain conditional on demonstrating the assumed noise in that quadrature. Oscillator locking, drift and technical phase noise remain decisive uncertainties.

2. JUNO: small delayed PMT signals persist into millisecond windows

Xiaojie Luo, Cailian Jiang, Haojie Dong, Yuduo Guan, Gaosong Li, Zhonghua Qin, Zhenning Qu, Junyu Shao, Mingxia Sun, Liangjian Wen, Zeyuan Yu and Boyi Zheng — “Long-Delayed Afterpulse Measurement of JUNO 20-inch Photomultiplier Tubes.” arXiv:2608.01943v2. First submitted 3 August 2026; v2 submitted 14 October, 06:52 UTC; 15 October listing. Submitted to JINST; no acceptance stated.

LED measurements of one dynode and one microchannel-plate PMT combine direct 1.8 ms records with sliding-window sampling to 20 ms. After dark-noise subtraction, the integrated 2–20 ms pulse-count yields per primary photoelectron are (8.1±0.4)×10−5 and (6.4±0.4)×10−4, respectively. These are measured yields, not exclusion limits; quoted uncertainties include mean single-photoelectron calibration.

Broad structures occur near 260 μs in the dynode tube and 90 and 550 μs in the MCP tube. Gain recovery can hide pulses below threshold, and the microscopic mechanism remains unresolved. Two tested tubes do not establish population-wide response. Methods, Figures 9–11 and discussion.

History

Afterpulses are correlated detector signals following a real light pulse; ordinary dark counts are largely uncorrelated. Earlier Zhao et al., arXiv:2207.04995, tested 150 MCP and 11 dynode PMTs, principally over 0.5–20 μs. Their average afterpulse-to-primary charge ratios were 5.6% and 13.2%, respectively.

Those percentages cannot be divided by the new pulse-count yields to infer an improvement: the observables, illumination and integration windows differ. The revised paper supplies later-time response information, rather than proving that afterpulsing has been eliminated. Physically, this matters because post-muon neutron-capture and veto analyses occupy similar delayed windows; translating a bench response into an in-detector energy bias still requires detector-level modeling. No oscillation-parameter shift is measured here.

3. Cluster gas motions in the CMB: evidence with a qualified interpretation

Samuel Goldstein and J. Colin Hill — “Evidence of galaxy cluster rotation and intrahalo gas motions in the cosmic microwave background.” arXiv:2512.10951v2. First submitted 11 December 2025; v2 submitted 13 October 2026, 22:13 UTC; 15 October listing. Updated to the published Physical Review Letters 137, 161001 version. This is a publication-version catch-up; the headline 3.6σ result was already in v1.

A weighted stack of 25 X-ray-selected clusters combines Planck temperature/polarization with SDSS galaxy velocities. Thermal-SZ removal and E-mode subtraction help isolate a rotation-aligned dipole. Random-position stacks give 3.6σ evidence. The fitted dimensionless template amplitude is ArkSZ=1.05±0.32 with a 68% posterior interval and uniform prior −5≤A≤5. The illustrative gas template fixes 1,500 km/s at 0.2r500c; its density profile is also fixed.

A randomized-angle diagnostic gives 2.9σ, but signal leaks into those realizations, so it is not a separately calibrated replacement significance. Mergers can contribute. Template agreement does not measure a unique gas rotation speed. Full analysis and robustness tests.

History

The kinematic SZ effect measures electron-density-weighted line-of-sight velocity through Thomson scattering of CMB photons. Opposite velocities across a cluster can create a temperature dipole; density structure, projection and mergers complicate its interpretation.

Baxter, Sherwin and Raghunathan (2019) used Planck maps and 13 clusters, with both parametric fits marginalizing electron-distribution parameters and a less model-dependent oriented stack, finding roughly 2σ evidence. The newer study strengthens this observational case with different selection, weighting and map processing. This is not a simple sensitivity-improvement factor between identical experiments. The physical decomposition into coherent rotation and other intrahalo flows remains uncertain.

Science News

1. EP261006a: MeerKAT constrains delayed radio emission

Announcement: 14 October 2026, 10:18 UTC; observations: 9 and 11 October. MeerKAT observed the 6 October Einstein Probe fast X-ray transient at 1.284 and 3.062 GHz. Combining the two epochs within each band, it found no radio source at the candidate optical counterpart, with preliminary 3σ limits of 39 and 27 μJy/beam, respectively. The epochs were approximately 3.1 and 5.1 days after the transient.

These constrain radio brightness at the specified position and times; they do not rule out an astrophysical transient, later radio emission, or a different source geometry. The team notes consistency with a contemporaneous VLA non-detection at 10 GHz. No neutrino or gravitational-wave association is established by this circular. GCN 45947.

2. GRB 261010A: an additional gamma-ray measurement, not a settled progenitor

Announcement: 14 October, 14:32 UTC; event: 10 October. Glowbug reports an onset at 19:17:01.824 UTC, an adaptive-window duration of 6.1 s, and total detection significance of approximately 26σ. The light curve has an initial spike and a brighter peak around 4.5 s later.

This updates the already-covered burst. The observation occurred in high radiation-belt background; the preliminary response does not fully characterize surrounding ISS passive material. The reported duration is not automatically interchangeable with another instrument's T90. Detection significance is evidence for the gamma-ray signal, not the probability of a compact-binary progenitor or either proposed host. GCN 45950.

Science Radar

1. Methane satellites face a year-long blind test

Frances E. Reuland and colleagues; peer-reviewed Nature publication: 14 October 2026, with an issue date of 15 October. A single-blind campaign tested 12 satellites using 481 metered methane releases, spanning 9.5–1,183.7 kg/hour, plus 265 blank controls. Fifteen teams supplied more than 2,250 estimates.

Purpose-built systems achieved 92–100% true-positive rates and 0–11% false-positive rates in the tested setting, with 44% mean absolute percentage error in quantification. Performance varied substantially across instruments and algorithms. AI-based methods produced 79% of the false positives; that is a share of the false positives in this study, not an AI false-positive rate of 79%.

The result is consequential because it tests detection against known releases and blanks rather than agreement between remote estimates. Source positions were approximately known, and cloud cover degraded performance: these results should not be generalized directly to unrestricted global searches or every landscape. Original paper; data and code.

2. Microplastic–water interfaces can be chemically active

Naixin Qian, Ruoqi Zhao and colleagues; peer-reviewed Nature publication and Columbia announcement: 14 October 2026. Laboratory assays and molecular calculations connect hydrophobic plastic–water interfaces to reactive oxygen species. The proposed mechanism involves interfacial hydroxide losing an electron more readily, producing reactive radicals.

This supplies a possible chemical route from particle exposure to biological damage. It does not quantify human disease risk at ordinary exposure levels or establish that every plastic behaves identically.

The published peer-review file records scrutiny of novelty, surfactant effects and radical identification. The authors report additional scavenger controls. Their supplement explicitly treats fluorescence-derived radical production as an operational lower-bound estimate, not an absolute total rate. The main article could not be retrieved in full; this account uses the accessible institutional announcement, supplement and review record. Paper; Columbia announcement.
