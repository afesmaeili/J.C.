ArXiv Highlights

The (missing) gamma-ray counterparts to EP events

Maria Edvige Ravasio, Ansh Chopra, Peter G. Jonker, Qinyu Wu, Eric Burns, Biswajit Banerjee, Gor Oganesyan, Stefano Ascenzi, Jesse T. Palmerio, Frederic Daigne, Joshua Wood, Rachel Hamburg, Andrew J. Levan, Jonathan A. Quirola-Vasquez, Javier Sanchez-Sierras, Joyce N. D. van Dalen, Agnes P. C. van Hoof, Hui Sun, Weimin Yuan, Nikhil Sarin, Antonio Martin-Carrillo, Dylan Adrien, Ce Cai, YanQiu Zhang, Samuele Ronchini and Marica Branchesi. arXiv:2610.28734v1, submitted 20 October, 18:00:02 UTC and newly listed 22 October. Accepted in Astronomy & Astrophysics. Systematic observational census with targeted searches and upper limits.

The physical question is whether Einstein Probe's fast X-ray transients (FXTs) without catalogued gamma-ray triggers are mostly ordinary gamma-ray bursts (GRBs) seen below existing monitor thresholds, intrinsically soft/weak relativistic explosions, or a mixture containing other transient classes. The authors search all 72 first-year EP FXTs in Fermi/GBM and LAT data, with complementary coverage from SVOM/GRM and ECLAIRs, Swift/BAT and Konus-Wind. Visibility is treated explicitly: 15 of the 23 counterpart events and 29 of the 49 nominal nondetections were in Fermi's usable field of view.

The census finds 23/72 (32%) gamma-ray counterparts, including seven subthreshold matches recovered by targeted analyses, and 49/72 (68%) with none. For detected events the gamma-to-X-ray flux ratio over the gamma-ray (T_{90}) lies roughly between 1 and 100 in the stated 10–1000 keV and 0.5–4 keV bands; using the whole, usually longer X-ray duration suppresses that ratio. For visible nondetections, simultaneous 3σ GBM limits constrain the most favorable cases to spectral peaks (E_{\rm peak}\lesssim30\text{–}100) keV. Among the 14 nondetections with redshift, inferred isotropic-equivalent luminosity limits span approximately (10^{48}) to (9\times10^{51}) erg s(^{-1}), depending on spectrum and event distance. No significant 0.1–1 GeV LAT signal is found for any visible FXT; the resulting gamma-to-X-ray flux-ratio limits are of order 1–10.

Mapping these limits to a launch Lorentz factor is model-dependent. If the classical GRB (E_{\rm peak})–luminosity–(\Gamma_0) correlations extend into this population, the lower energetics imply (\Gamma_0\lesssim100) in the best-constrained cases; this is not a direct kinematic measurement. The paper's synchrotron-self-Compton calculations are likewise exploratory because magnetic field, emission radius, Lorentz factor and electron distribution are poorly constrained. Selection effects are substantial: gamma monitors have nonuniform sky exposure and energy response, and EP selects on soft X-rays. Therefore the observed 32/68 split is not an intrinsic population fraction. The study supports a continuous extension of the GRB population, but does not prove that every gamma-quiet FXT is a GRB. Full text, sample table and Figures 2–10.

History

The established baseline is that EP's wide-field soft-X-ray sensitivity uncovers bright, rapidly evolving events for which conventional hard-X-ray monitors often supply no trigger. Optical work on the same first-year sample followed 42 of 72 FXTs, found 12 optical counterparts and identified 11 direct GRB associations; that study established the importance of afterglows but could not determine the prompt gamma-ray properties of most events. A separate redshift-distribution comparison found no statistically significant difference between EP FXTs and long GRBs, a population-level consistency test rather than an event classifier. Individual cases such as EP240414a already showed that a gamma nondetection can accommodate several physical scenarios.

This paper changes the baseline by uniformly interrogating the keV–GeV archives and by recovering seven previously subthreshold counterparts, then turning genuine coverage with no counterpart into spectral and luminosity constraints. Earlier GRB correlations motivate the inferred low-(\Gamma_0) interpretation, but extrapolating them to soft, low-luminosity events remains the leading uncertainty. The principal unresolved question is mixture: a continuous GRB tail, off-axis or choked jets, shock breakout and non-GRB transients can overlap in EP observables. Larger redshift-complete samples and contemporaneous broad-band coverage are needed before the gamma-quiet fraction can be assigned to physical classes.

Interpreting the gamma-ray emission from Westerlund 1: hadronic or leptonic?

Ben Li, Pasquale Blasi and Elena Amato. arXiv:2610.28951v1, submitted 20 October, 22:04:13 UTC and newly listed 22 October. Preprint marked “Submitted”; 13 pages, six figures. Transport and emission modeling, not a new gamma-ray detection.

The paper asks whether the extended GeV–TeV source around the massive stellar cluster Westerlund 1 is dominated by pion decay from accelerated protons or inverse-Compton emission from relativistic electrons. Particles are injected at the cluster-wind termination shock; steady transport equations are solved for protons and primary/secondary electrons with advection, losses and either Bohm or Kraichnan diffusion. The resulting (pp), inverse-Compton and synchrotron emission is fitted jointly to Fermi-LAT and H.E.S.S. spectra and morphology, with the eROSITA X-ray nondetection constraining synchrotron radiation.

For Bohm diffusion, both a proton-dominated and an electron-dominated solution reproduce the multiwavelength data, so the usual hadronic/leptonic degeneracy survives. With Kraichnan diffusion, which the authors argue is more plausible for cluster turbulence, the leptonic spectrum falls short of the highest-energy gamma-ray points whereas the hadronic solution remains viable. In the leptonic case eROSITA restricts the immediately downstream magnetic field to no more than a few microgauss. In a purely hadronic interpretation with at most 10% of the wind power in accelerated protons, the bubble-averaged target density must be (n\gtrsim1.2\ { m cm}^{-3}). Their worked hadronic model takes (n=2\ { m cm}^{-3}), proton efficiency about 0.06 and electron-to-proton injection ratio (K_{ep}\sim10^{-3}).

That density requirement is the critical caveat. A smooth shocked wind gives only about 0.02 cm(^{-3}), and thermal evaporation estimates nearer 0.2 cm(^{-3}) still fall short; cold entrained clumps or other gas phases must supply the effective (pp) target. Conversely, the leptonic solution needs weak post-shock fields and a diffusion prescription capable of retaining sufficiently energetic electrons. The predicted KM3NeT discovery significance of roughly 3.8σ after ten years for the benchmark hadronic model is a forecast conditional on that density, spectrum and detector response—not a present neutrino signal. Spatially resolved gas, X-ray and future TeV data remain more discriminating than the current broadband fit alone. Full text, Figures 2–6.

History

H.E.S.S. discovered HESS J1646−458 as a degree-scale source at about 20σ, with photon index (2.19\pm0.08_{\rm stat}\pm0.20_{\rm sys}) and integral flux above 0.2 TeV of ((5.2\pm0.9)\times10^{-11}\ { m cm}^{-2}{\rm s}^{-1}). Westerlund 1 was favored as a plausible accelerator, but source multiplicity was not excluded. A later termination-shock model by Härer et al. preferred a leptonic interpretation and found that hadrons faced confinement and energy-budget difficulties. Meanwhile, eROSITA found no diffuse synchrotron counterpart and quoted a model-dependent 1σ magnetic-field upper bound of 7 μG for a fully leptonic scenario; hadronic, leptonic and hybrid explanations still survived.

The new work revisits that conflict with coupled proton/electron transport and explicit morphology. Its main advance is not a categorical hadronic identification but the result that the answer changes with the diffusion law: Bohm leaves both branches open, Kraichnan removes the fitted leptonic branch. Proposed neutrino tests are also not yet decisive: a recent Baikal-GVD cluster study reported three events near Westerlund 1 but found the excess compatible with an accidental post-trial fluctuation. The remaining uncertainty is dominated by transport and the unresolved multiphase gas density, so “hadronic favored under Kraichnan” is more accurate than “hadronic proven.”

Searches for coincident Gravitational Waves and High-Energy Neutrinos from the KM3NeT and ANTARES telescopes during O3

ANTARES Collaboration and KM3NeT Collaboration (O. Adriani et al.). arXiv:2610.29436v1, submitted 21 October, 08:59:23 UTC and newly listed 22 October. Collaboration preprint; no journal reference listed; 41 pages, 15 figures. Null search and conditional rate constraints.

The analysis tests whether transient engines produced both gravitational waves (GWs) and high-energy neutrinos during LIGO–Virgo's third observing run. It uses two complementary directions. First, four selected ANTARES neutrino candidates trigger minimally modeled coherent GW searches with X-Pipeline in a ±500 s window over 20–500 Hz. Second, subthreshold compact-binary GW candidates are combined with ANTARES and six-line KM3NeT/ORCA neutrino candidates using spatial overlap and ±30 s and ±500 s coincidence windows.

No association passes the prespecified threshold. In the ±500 s joint search, 34 ANTARES and 36 ORCA6 coincidences survive initial selection; the most significant inverse false-alarm rates are only 0.4 yr and 0.07 yr, respectively. The ±30 s search retains three and two coincidences, with maxima 0.8 yr and 0.2 yr. All are below the required inverse false-alarm rate of 1 yr, and inspection associates the leading cases with terrestrial GW triggers or noise-compatible neutrinos. The four neutrino-triggered searches also show no GW excess. Model-specific 90% exclusion distances for standard-candle mergers range from 22.6–72.0 Mpc for binary neutron stars and 49.9–157.6 Mpc for neutron-star–black-hole systems.

For a fiducial binary-neutron-star population emitting (E_{\rm GW}=0.1M_\odot c^2) and isotropic-equivalent neutrino energy (E_{\nu,\rm iso}=10^{51}) erg with the adopted spectrum, the fully joint search gives a 90% confidence source-rate density upper limit of (8.8\times10^2\ { m Gpc}^{-3}{\rm yr}^{-1}). This is not a universal bound: it scales with assumed GW and neutrino energies, spectrum, orientation, sky exposure and source class. The triggered-search rate construction additionally assumes all four input neutrinos are astrophysical and adopts beaming factor (f_b=10), deliberately conservative choices that preclude direct comparison with rates conditioned on confirmed astrophysical events. Full text, Tables 1–4 and Figures 4–15.

History

Joint GW–neutrino searches began with high-profile events such as GW150914, for which IceCube and ANTARES found no coincident high-energy neutrino. ANTARES later searched individually around public O2 and O3 GW candidates; its O3 targeted analysis found no excess around 80 GW events and set stacked, spectrum-dependent neutrino-energy bounds, including values of order (4\times10^{53}) erg divided by the neutrino-emitting fraction for binary black holes. Those were GW-triggered fluence constraints, not the same population-rate observable reported here.

The new analysis adds the reverse search—neutrinos triggering a coherent burst search—and a fully joint subthreshold statistic using both ANTARES and ORCA6. This expands sensitivity to sources too weak to enter either standalone catalogue. It confirms the previous null picture rather than conflicting with it. Numerical “improvement factors” would be misleading because detector configurations, trigger populations, spectra, time windows and population assumptions differ. The open uncertainty is astrophysical: neutrino energies and beaming from mergers, collapsars and disk instabilities span orders of magnitude, while O3 ORCA was still in a six-line construction phase.

How general relativity stacks up: Testing gravity by coherently combining gravitational-wave observations

Lachlan Passenger, Nir Guttman, Paul D. Lasky and Eric Thrane. arXiv:2610.29247v1, submitted 21 October, 05:51:12 UTC and newly listed 22 October. Preprint; 23 pages, seven figures and one table. Population-level consistency method and null result.

Rather than fit one modified-gravity coefficient to each merger, the authors ask whether coherent residual strain appears near merger and varies smoothly with source-frame total mass. They select 28 binary-black-hole signals from GWTC-5 with network signal-to-noise ratio at least 15, analyze Hanford and Livingston data, and concentrate on the dominant (\ell=|m|=2) mode in the 50–511 Hz ringdown-sensitive band. Baseline maximum-likelihood waveforms use IMRPhenomXPHM for earlier events and SpinTaylor-based models for newer catalog entries.

Possible deviations in the plus and cross polarizations are modeled as functions of time and total mass with a Gaussian process. The analysis fixes a mass coherence scale (\Delta M=10M_\odot) and regularization hyperparameters after injection studies, then combines event residuals rather than incoherent per-event (p)-values. Against 97 empirical noise catalogues, the observed population statistic is (\ln\lambda_{\rm pop}=0.46) with (p=0.05). The authors interpret this as consistent with GR. Given only 97 background realizations, the tail calibration is coarse, and 0.05 should not be promoted into evidence for a deviation.

This is a deliberately restricted null test, not a generic bound on modified gravity. It assumes deviations peak near merger, depend smoothly on total mass and primarily enter the quadrupole. A sharp resonance, higher-mode anomaly, propagation effect or parameter dependence other than mass can evade it. The proposed diagnostic—mass-independent residuals suggest waveform systematics whereas smooth mass-dependent residuals could suggest new physics—is useful but heuristic, because waveform errors can also depend on mass and spin. No coupling constant, energy scale or confidence interval on a named theory is reported. Full text, Figures 2–7.

History

Catalog-level strong-field tests have repeatedly found consistency with GR. The GWTC-3 tests combined inspiral–merger–ringdown consistency, parametrized phasing, dispersion, ringdown and residual analyses without a significant departure. The LVK GWTC-5 tests extend that baseline with seven families of tests and likewise report no compelling deviation. Hierarchical population approaches have also shown that flexible treatment of event-to-event variation can prevent one outlier from dominating a combined result.

The present method changes what is combined: it reconstructs a coherent strain deviation shared smoothly across a source property rather than multiplying posteriors for a fixed theory coefficient or stacking scalar significances. That can expose a population pattern while retaining time-domain morphology. It does not overturn the catalog consensus; it supplies a new consistency projection with its own blind spots. More noise realizations, spins and mass ratios as covariates, higher modes and independent waveform families are needed before a marginal population statistic could be interpreted physically.

Testing the statistical compatibility of data sets with a common prior

Pilar Coloma, Michele Maltoni and Thomas Schwetz. arXiv:2610.28681v1, submitted 19 October, 18:00:27 UTC and newly listed 22 October. Methodological preprint with report number IFT-UAM/CSIC-26-135; 26 pages, six figures, one table and accompanying Python code.

The Parameter Goodness-of-fit (PG) test asks whether statistically independent data sets prefer compatible values of a model's shared parameters, while avoiding dilution by bins that carry little parameter information. A common auxiliary constraint—called a prior here—breaks the independence assumption because it is reused in every fit. The paper derives two corrected statistics, (\mathrm{PG}\star) and (\mathrm{PG}+), and their null distributions in the Gaussian approximation.

(\mathrm{PG}+) is non-negative and assigns greater weight to tension between the common prior and the data; (\mathrm{PG}\star) more closely isolates mutual data-set tension but can be negative. In general neither follows an ordinary chi-square law: the distribution is a generalized (\chi^2) obtained from eigenvalues of covariance-matrix combinations. In the strong-prior limit both approach the standard PG statistic for the remaining unconstrained parameters. In the weak-prior limit (\mathrm{PG}+) approaches an ordinary chi-square with the combined relevant dimensions, whereas even a simple (\mathrm{PG}\star) case becomes the difference of two chi-square variables.

Monte Carlo tests with (10^4) neutrino-oscillation-like pseudoexperiments validate the analytic calibration in high-statistics, single-minimum examples. The code turns supplied covariance information into survival probabilities, but the demonstration is not a measurement of tension in any real experiment. The caveats are explicit: Gaussian likelihoods, interior parameters, a unique local minimum and known covariance matrices. Physical boundaries, degeneracies, multimodal oscillation likelihoods, nonlinear nuisance responses and empirically estimated priors may require dedicated simulation. Full text and ancillary code.

History

Maltoni and Schwetz introduced the PG test in 2003 to diagnose whether independent experiments agree in the parameter subspace they jointly constrain. It became standard in neutrino-global-fit tension studies because a global goodness-of-fit can look excellent when many insensitive bins swamp a small incompatible subset. Cosmology developed related diagnostics for correlated probes and shared calibration information, but applying the original PG formula after duplicating one prior can overstate or understate disagreement.

The new result supplies an analytic correction for exactly that shared-prior geometry and separates two reasonable questions: data-to-data compatibility and joint data-to-prior compatibility. A timely example is atmospheric-neutrino combinations that share flux or cross-section constraints; the independent ordering analysis highlighted on 21 October reported a parameter-goodness (p=0.014), but that specific number cannot be revised without reimplementing its likelihood under the new prescription. The paper therefore changes the statistical toolbox, not any published experimental conclusion. Its practical value will depend on real analyses releasing enough covariance and likelihood information to test the Gaussian approximation.

IceCube-261020A: Fermi-LAT finds no significant gamma-ray excess

The Bronze-track neutrino candidate occurred 20 October at 03:58:53.39 UTC; IceCube's offline reconstruction placed it at RA (224.12\pm0.56^\circ), Dec (+20.62\pm0.61^\circ) (quoted 90% PSF containment) with atmospheric-background false-alarm rate 2.108 yr(^{-1}). A Fermi-LAT follow-up circulated 21 October at 11:09:47 UTC reports no new source above 100 MeV at (>5σ) in the localization. Assuming a point source with photon index 2, the 95% photon-flux upper limits are (4.6\times10^{-10} { m cm}^{-2}{\rm s}^{-1}) over the full 2008-to-trigger interval, (7.2\times10^{-9}) over the preceding month and (7.2\times10^{-8}) over the preceding day. GCN 46004. This is a gamma-ray nondetection for specified windows and spectrum, not evidence that the neutrino is atmospheric and not proof that no transient counterpart existed.

EP261015a: two late optical measurements support slow evolution

Following the correction reported in the previous edition, AZT-20 measured the proposed optical counterpart at (r'=22.59\pm0.06) AB on 19 October at 19:20 UTC, 4.2764 days after the EP trigger; the circular was issued 21 October at 06:17:10 UTC. GCN 46003. DFOT then obtained a tentative difference-image detection of (R=22.30\pm0.24) AB on 20 October, 5.25484 days after trigger. GCN 46006. The latter is uncorrected for Galactic extinction and sensitive to template-subtraction systematics. Together with the corrected COLIBRÍ and Wendelstein points, these measurements favor a slowly evolving optical source; they do not by themselves identify a supernova, afterglow or other physical counterpart class.

GRB 261020B: AstroSat/CZTI detects a 0.11-second hard pulse

AstroSat/CZTI reports a short burst peaking 20 October at 14:52:41.55 UTC, with (T_{90}=0.11^{+0.11}_{-0.03}) s in 20–200 keV. The background-subtracted total is (108\pm30) counts and the 100-ms peak count rate is (1150^{+236}_{-249}) s(^{-1}) above background; the anticoincidence detector also saw the event in 100–500 keV. GCN 46005, issued 21 October. Duration makes it a short-GRB candidate, but this instrumental detection alone provides neither an astrophysical localization nor evidence for a compact-binary merger or gravitational-wave association.

Science Radar

Reducing excess asparagine synthetase rescues several Angelman-like phenotypes in mice

A peer-reviewed Nature article published 21 October uses gain- and loss-of-function UBE3A variants, ubiquitin-remnant proteomics and biochemical validation to identify asparagine synthetase (ASNS) as a direct UBE3A substrate. Loss of neuronal UBE3A causes Angelman syndrome; the authors find excess ASNS in Ube3a-null mouse brain and in previously generated human patient-derived neurons. In mice, genetically removing one Asns copy brings ASNS nearer wild-type levels and improves microcephaly, obesity, center-time, hindlimb-clasping and marble-burying phenotypes.

Mechanistically, p-eIF2α—a marker of the integrated stress response—rises to (176.43\pm7.13)% of wild type in Angelman-model brain and falls to (140.56\pm10.94)% in the genetic rescue. Translation efficiency measured by polysome profiling improves from (72.94\pm2.83)% to (95.08\pm5.12)% of wild type. The causal chain is strong for this mouse model, but incomplete: rotarod performance and abnormal sleep do not improve, pointing to additional UBE3A substrates. Human evidence comes from cell models, not a treatment trial; partial ASNS reduction also needs safety study because complete ASNS deficiency causes severe neurodevelopmental disease. The result nominates ASNS/HRI/stress signaling as targets, not a clinically established therapy.

A nine-feature blood multi-omic classifier for long COVID is promising but not externally validated

A peer-reviewed early-version Scientific Reports article published 21 October compares peripheral-blood proteomics, metabolomics and leukocyte RNA sequencing from 155 people with self-reported post-acute sequelae of COVID-19 (PASC), 100 acutely ill COVID-19 patients and 44 healthy controls. Integrated analyses implicate persistent departures from healthy molecular states, including sphingolipid and chromatin-associated pathways. A machine-learning model using nine selected entities reports mean precision–recall AUC 0.97 in the authors' resampling framework.

That performance is exploratory, not yet a diagnostic accuracy estimate for clinical practice. There is no independent external validation cohort, feature selection and assessment occur within the same overall study, PASC status is self-reported, and acute-COVID controls differ from a post-acute clinic population by disease phase as well as symptoms. Batch, treatment, vaccination, variant and demographic effects may also be encoded in the omics. The open data resource is useful for replication and meta-analysis, but a prospectively enrolled, time-matched cohort with prespecified thresholds is required before claiming a nine-marker diagnostic.
