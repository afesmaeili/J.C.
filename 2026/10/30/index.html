ArXiv Highlights

1. Can galaxy halos retain a cosmological reservoir of cosmic rays?

“Cosmic rays in galaxy halos I: the circumgalactic transport rate and a dynamically significant cosmic-ray reservoir” — Roland M. Crocker and Mark R. Krumholz. arXiv:2610.38061v1, full text. Submitted 27 October 2026, 23:23:28 UTC; first listed 30 October. Preprint, explicitly circulated for feedback before journal submission; primary category astro-ph.HE.

Most cosmic-ray protons accelerated in ordinary star-forming discs escape before colliding. The question is whether they then leave the galaxy's gaseous halo, heat that gas, radiate through pion production, or accumulate for billions of years. The distinction matters for both galaxy evolution and the origin of the isotropic gamma-ray background, or IGRB.

The authors evolve a spherical transport model from redshift 8 to 0, including halo growth, star-formation-dependent injection, diffusion, hadronic losses and streaming heating. They compare reflecting virial-shock boundaries with free escape, while varying the gas-density slope and the dimensionless transport coefficient K:

D=K3rvircs,tdiff=rvir26D.

The independent checks are the 1–10 GeV Fermi IGRB, outer-Milky-Way gamma-ray emissivities, 0.5–2 keV eROSITA halo luminosities, hydrostatic support and the pressure that an accretion shock can confine. Figures 5–6 show how these requirements remove different parts of parameter space.

One surviving regime has K≃0.05–0.2, corresponding to D≃(2–7)×1029cm2s−1 for a present-day 1012M⊙ halo. The implied crossing time is approximately 0.5–2 Hubble times. In this regime a halo retains roughly 1058 erg in cosmic-ray protons; the retained fraction spans approximately 27–59% of the energy injected into the CGM across the allowed slow-transport regions. This is different from the fraction of all cosmic-ray production, because disc losses occur first. Halo streaming can also supply the heating associated with the observed X-ray luminosity. Rapid free escape remains an allowed alternative.

These are conditional model ranges, not confidence intervals or a detected reservoir. The scan fixes the cosmic-ray yield at 1050 erg per core-collapse supernova, the injection spectral index at 2.2, vA/cs=0.1, and an energy-averaged transport prescription. It does not marginalize over these assumptions. Its IGRB rejection threshold, 1.82×10−8ergcm−2s−1 integrated over the sky in 1–10 GeV, combines three statistical errors with an upward foreground systematic; it is not a calibrated joint “3σ” bound. A radius-dependent diffusion coefficient can trade against the gas profile. The X-ray heating condition also depends on streaming losses and the adopted bolometric correction.

History

The reservoir idea predates this paper. Feldmann, Hooper and Gnedin, arXiv:1205.0249, published in ApJ 763, 21 (2013), coupled a simulated ionized-gas halo to cosmic-ray diffusion and estimated a 3–10% Milky Way contribution to the high-latitude gamma-ray background for their illustrated transport choices. Reading their Section 4 reveals an important qualification to the new paper's comparison: the earlier calculation already used a time-dependent source term over 10 Gyr, including stronger past star formation. It should not simply be described as a steady-state calculation. The new work's advance is the coupled halo evolution, losses and simultaneous energetic/dynamical tests; its larger halo contribution cannot be attributed to adding time dependence alone. Different gas profiles, energy bands and background definitions also prevent a clean improvement factor.

A sharper comparison is Butsky et al., arXiv:2210.14232, MNRAS 521, 2477 (2023). Their COS-Halos analysis derives a lower limit on the radially averaged total effective transport rate, including advection, from hydrogen columns, star formation and circular velocity. It favors κeff≳1030–1031cm2s−1 at 50–100 kpc and beyond, for pressure-dominating approximately GeV protons. This is not the same parameter as a constant diffusion coefficient relative to the gas. Its equilibrium/injection assumptions and column-density uncertainties differ, and the plotted limits are not a uniform 95% confidence construction. Nevertheless, the tension with a low, radius-independent coefficient is substantive; it remains unresolved by a simple numerical comparison.

Finally, Chen and Totani, arXiv:2502.19879, independently found that star-forming galaxies supply only about 50–60% of the unresolved background near 1–10 GeV under their calibration. They examined stellar-initial-mass-function consistency and other normalization issues. The new paper explicitly applies related corrections in Appendix B and assigns its disc component about 60%. Its remaining halo contribution is therefore a testable interpretation, rather than proof that other populations such as unresolved active galaxies are unnecessary.

2. SBND's first argon cross sections expose complementary nuclear-model failures

“Measurements of transverse kinematic imbalance in muon neutrino charged-current interactions on argon with a single proton and no pions in the final state with SBND” — R. Acciarri et al. (SBND Collaboration). arXiv:2610.39042v1, paper and supplement. Submitted 28 October 2026, 23:15:33 UTC; first listed 30 October. Preprint, with no journal reference stated; primary category hep-ex.

Oscillation experiments infer neutrino properties through interactions inside nuclei. Fermi motion, two-body currents and the rescattering of outgoing particles can change reconstructed energies and imitate changes in the incoming neutrino spectrum. SBND tests these effects using a particularly clean final state: one muon and one proton, with no detectable pions.

The exposure is 8.8×1019 protons on target, at mean neutrino energy 0.75 GeV. The analysis selects 12,804 candidates, with 91% simulated signal purity. Its signal definition requires muon momentum 0.22–1.0 GeV/c and proton momentum 0.3–1.0 GeV/c; extra protons above 0.3 GeV/c, charged pions above 70 MeV/c, neutral pions and heavier mesons are excluded. Neutrons are unrestricted. This topology includes non-quasielastic processes that produce the same final state; the simulation estimates an 80% quasielastic component.

The central observable is

δpT=|pTμ+pTp|.

For scattering from a stationary free nucleon, transverse momentum balances. Nuclear motion and rescattering broaden the distribution. The angles δαT and δϕT add sensitivity to the direction of the imbalance and departure from muon–proton coplanarity.

SBND unfolds differential cross sections with Wiener-SVD regularization and supplies covariance and regularization matrices. Theory must be folded with the supplied AC matrix before comparison. The integrated systematic uncertainty is 8.7%, dominated by the 6.5% flux component; detector and interaction-model contributions are 3.7% and 3.4%. Differential shapes remain more discriminating than the normalization.

For δpT, Figure 2 gives full-covariance χ2/ndof values 33.6/12 for GENIE AR23, 63.6/12 for GiBUU 2025 and 62.4/12 for NEUT 6.1.4. None describes that distribution satisfactorily. GENIE tends to undershoot the peak/intermediate region and overshoot the high tail. Yet GENIE describes the angular distributions substantially better: 3.9/8 for δαT and 11.6/11 for δϕT. Hartree–Fock variants improve the momentum imbalance but worsen the angular slope. No tested configuration succeeds across all three observables.

This is a cross-section measurement, not a new-physics or oscillation signal. Acceptance corrections depend on simulation, although the supplement tests alternate-generator fake data, smooth deformations and injected spectral bumps. A model's failure does not uniquely identify whether its initial nuclear state, primary interaction or final-state transport needs revision.

History

The direct predecessor is MicroBooNE's first double-differential kinematic-imbalance measurement, arXiv:2301.03706, Physical Review Letters 131, 101802 (2023). With 6.79×1020 protons on target, it selected 9,051 events with approximately 70% purity. Its double-differential distributions separated regions with weak and strong final-state interactions: the high-δpT, high-δαT region strongly disfavored predictions with rescattering switched off. Nuclear effects were already established; SBND makes their detailed modeling harder to evade with a cleaner independent argon sample.

The samples are not interchangeable. MicroBooNE accepted muons at 0.1–1.2 GeV/c, versus SBND's 0.22–1.0 GeV/c, and allowed additional protons above 1 GeV/c outside its selected-proton window; SBND vetoes extra protons above 0.3 GeV/c. Generator versions and tunes also changed. A purity or uncertainty ratio is therefore not a universal improvement factor, and the different exposures do not compare fluxes or target masses directly.

SBND confirms the earlier difficulty with the momentum-imbalance peak and the tendency of GiBUU to predict too steep an angular-imbalance slope. It also finds that some form-factor modifications that improved comparisons with MicroBooNE do not achieve comparable agreement here. The practical advance is an additional, precisely specified benchmark for neutrino–argon modeling relevant to SBN and DUNE. The remaining question is whether one common interaction model can describe both experiments after applying their distinct signal definitions and response matrices.

Science News

GRB 261029A: GeV photons, an X-ray counterpart and a possible optical plateau

Substantive follow-up to the 29 October report. The burst began on 29 October at 01:27:03.81 UTC. Fermi/GBM reports T90≃43.8 s in 50–300 keV. A preliminary Band-function fit over −5.9 to +86.9 s gives Epeak=621±7 keV and a 10–1000 keV fluence of (1.100±0.009)×10−4ergcm−2. GCN 46098.

Fermi/LAT detects emission above 100 MeV, with (9.4±1.1)×10−4photonscm−2s−1 over the first 120 s; the highest-energy photon is 2.5 GeV at +44 s. Its position is RA 9.69°, Dec 35.52°, with 0.57° statistical 90% containment. The circular describes high significance but supplies no numerical test statistic or false-alarm rate. GCN 46085.

Einstein Probe/FXT began observing at 02:14 UTC, about 47 minutes after the trigger, and found a source 1.67 arcsec from the optical counterpart. Its 0.5–10 keV flux is (2.822±0.128)×10−11ergcm−2s−1, with a quoted 1σ error and a 10-arcsec localization radius whose confidence level is not specified. GCN 46086.

FRAM reports fading from 13.30±0.03 to 17.42±0.10 AB between 12 and 138 minutes, in unfiltered photometry calibrated to r. Later Ondřejov data suggest a plateau or rebrightening near i≃19.6 AB at 0.75 day. These preliminary light curves do not yet uniquely establish renewed central-engine activity or a particular afterglow mechanism. GCN 46097; 46102.

Corrections: GCN 46084 corrects the LCO report's event identification to GRB 261029A. The second MIRAGE infrared report, 46093, again prints 27 October while describing observations hours after the 29 October burst. Its authors report roughly 2.2 mag fading, but the absolute infrared chronology remains inconsistent and is not silently repaired here.

GRB 261024D: interplanetary timing produces a narrow, elongated localization

The 29 October, 15:17 UTC IPN update triangulates the long burst of 24 October, approximately 21:33:02 UTC, using detections across multiple spacecraft, including Konus-Wind and Mars Odyssey. The preliminary 3σ error box covers 1.1 square degrees, centered at RA 212.913°, Dec 31.361°. Its maximum dimension is 15°, but its minimum width is only 4 arcmin: a circular radius would misrepresent the search region. GCN 46094 and localization map.

This is a localization advance for an existing gamma-ray event, not a new burst or counterpart association. The position was only 36° from the Sun, complicating follow-up; the team says the localization may improve.

EP-WXT trigger 01709321980: optical follow-up supports a stellar-flare identification

Einstein Probe triggered on 30 October at 05:23:34 UTC. Its team associated the event with ATO J336.9854+01.6911, reporting an approximate 0.5–4 keV flux of 8×10−11ergcm−2s−1. GCN 46106.

BOOTES-5 began at 05:30 UTC and found that star inside the reported FXT error circle, fading by 0.18 mag in 12 minutes in clear-filter observations. The team identifies the trigger as a stellar flare. The coordinated variability strengthens that identification beyond positional coincidence alone; neither circular provides a numerical false-association probability. GCN 46107, issued 06:43 UTC.

Swift science operations have ended; CHIME's alert interruption is separate

A 29 October GCN service announcement confirms that Swift obtained its final observations on 20 October. NASA's original announcement was dated 23 October, so the mission-ending event is catch-up, not a new 30 October development. NASA reports that the low orbit and atmospheric drag prevent stable science pointing. Archival processing continues. Atmospheric re-entry was still a forecast in these sources, not a verified completed event. NASA announcement; GCN service notice.

The same notice says CHIME/FRB GCN alerts have been offline since 18 October at 17:00 UTC for a hardware upgrade, with return expected in January 2027. That is a planned alert-service interruption with an estimated end date. Missing Swift or CHIME alerts must therefore not be interpreted as evidence that the transient sky is quiet.

Science Radar

Medical reasoning models: better primary diagnoses, more unsupported diagnoses

Shuai Wu et al., “The accuracy-safety trade-off of large reasoning models in clinical diagnostics,” Nature Communications, published 29 October 2026. This is a peer-reviewed, accepted Article in Press, still subject to final production edits. Article; accepted full text.

The retrospective benchmark compares six matched reasoning/non-reasoning model pairs across electronic health records. In 1,157 MIMIC-derived cases, average primary-diagnosis accuracy was 52.8% versus 38.7%, while false-positive diagnoses increased from 1.09 to 1.47 per case—a 34.8% relative increase. Both accuracy and error definitions depend on the benchmark's diagnostic labels.

A 15-physician study found roughly 20% relative improvement in primary accuracy with assistance and about 44% fewer false positives than the standalone reasoning model. These have different comparison baselines; neither is a percentage-point change.

The limitation is decisive: this was retrospective full-episode diagnosis with a restricted 40-disease candidate list, not a prospective trial of patient outcomes. Administrative-code labels can be imperfect, and later hospital information can aid predictions. The finding supports evaluating diagnostic breadth and reliability separately; it does not demonstrate that autonomous deployment is safe.

Agricultural expansion increasingly falls in tropical and dryland environments

Lun Gao, Xiaoman Lu, Weston Anderson, Corey Lesk et al., “Global arable land redistribution toward the tropics and drylands under human settlement expansion,” Nature Communications, published 29 October 2026. Peer-reviewed, accepted Article in Press. Article; accepted full text.

The study combines five land-cover products with FAO statistics to construct 1-km arable-land maps for 2000, 2010 and 2020, then compares them with settlement footprints and climate zones. Estimated global arable area increased from 12.3 to 13.1 million km² during 2000–2020; 83% of net gains occurred in tropical and arid zones. Global gains can therefore coexist with losses around established settlements and greater pressure on water-limited or biodiverse regions.

“Arable land” here excludes permanent woody crops and shifting cultivation; it is not all agricultural land. Settlement footprints omit roads, and three mapping epochs cannot resolve every transition. The spatial analysis supports a redistribution pattern, but does not establish that each hectare gained elsewhere was causally displaced by urban growth. Policy, trade, hydrology and local economic conditions remain additional drivers.
