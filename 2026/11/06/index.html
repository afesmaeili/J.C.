01First broadband search for fixed-polarization dark photon dark matter with GigaBREADJialin Yu, Stefan Knirck, Gabe Hoshino, Rakshya Khatiwada, Alex Lapuente, David W. Miller and Andrew Sonnenschein

GigaBREAD uses Earth's rotation to search for a fixed dark-photon polarization

Paper: First broadband search for fixed-polarization dark photon dark matter with GigaBREAD — Jialin Yu, Stefan Knirck, Gabe Hoshino, Rakshya Khatiwada, Alex Lapuente, David W. Miller and Andrew Sonnenschein. arXiv:2611.07426v1, full text. Submitted 5 November 2026 at 04:20:25 UTC and first listed 6 November. v1 Fermilab preprint, report FERMILAB-PUB-26-0792-ETD-PPD; no journal publication is listed. Classification: experimental reanalysis with Bayesian credible and frequentist confidence limits; no dark-photon detection.

Parameter and signal. A dark photon of mass (m_{\rm DP}) kinetically mixed with the ordinary photon by the dimensionless parameter (chi) can convert into a radio photon at a conducting surface. Unlike axions, this conversion needs no applied magnetic field. If vector dark matter was produced by a misalignment-like mechanism, its polarization may remain approximately fixed in celestial coordinates. The signal in a detector sensitive along the axis D^(t) is then

Pdet(t)=P0|X^·D^(t)|2,

so Earth's sidereal rotation supplies a predictable modulation rather than the constant factor ⟨cos2ϑ⟩=1/3 used for random polarization.

Data and analysis. GigaBREAD converts dark photons on a vertical cylindrical conductor of effective area about 0.5 m² and focuses the emitted 10.7–12.5 GHz radiation into a horn. The reanalysis combines a 24-day, zenith-aligned University of Chicago run with 12,970 spectra and a 27-day, horizontally aligned Argonne run with 12,481 spectra. Each spectrum contains 230,392 bins at 7.8 kHz resolution. The reference conversion assumes a local dark-matter density of 0.45 GeV cm⁻³.

The likelihood retains each spectrum's time stamp and matches both the frequency profile and the sidereal polarization pattern. Because absolute timing was not preserved across the 2023 sub-runs, the polarization azimuth is marginalized independently for each sub-run, while the celestial latitude remains common. Bayesian evidence and a profile-likelihood statistic are calibrated with 300 full noise simulations, covering 1,151,965 mass templates. The effective frequentist trials factor is about 5×105. Limits incorporate a 23% power systematic inherited from the detector calibration.

Result. The observed global Bayesian statistic is k0global=−2.5, below the paper's 90% candidate threshold of −2.3; the frequentist scan likewise contains no globally significant mass. The authors report conservative limits by taking the weaker of their Bayesian 90% credible and frequentist 90% confidence upper limits at every mass. Over 44–52 μeV, the resulting fixed-polarization limit is at the level of

χ~10−12.

It is approximately 20% lower in χ than a naive fixed-polarization rescaling of the earlier GigaBREAD result and is comparable to, or slightly stronger than, the earlier random-polarization limit. The improvement comes from using the timing information and a second detector orientation. It applies to a fixed but unknown polarization; it is not a general improvement for every dark-photon production model.

Limitations. The result assumes that polarization stays coherent and fixed over the observation span and that the Standard Halo Model describes the spectral line. The missing absolute phase across 2023 sub-runs removes some directional information. Instrumental baselines are removed with a Savitzky–Golay filter, and the analysis relies on Monte Carlo calibration of a non-Wilks statistic. The constraint also scales with the assumed local density and conversion efficiency. No mass exceeded the candidate threshold, so the work supplies exclusions rather than evidence for a modulated signal.

History

The original GigaBREAD search used the 24-day 2023 data set and treated dark-photon polarization as random. It excluded roughly χ≳10−12 at 90% confidence over the same 44–52 μeV interval and improved previous bounds by about two orders of magnitude below 49 μeV (Knirck et al., arXiv:2310.13891, Physical Review Letters 132, 131004). The new paper does not gain an order of magnitude or open a new mass range. Its substantive advance is a statistically consistent reinterpretation for fixed polarization, plus the combination of vertical and horizontal runs.

Dish searches such as SHUKET, QUALIPHIDE, DOSUE and BRASS-p constrain overlapping or neighboring masses with different receivers, geometries and polarization conventions. Resonant haloscopes including HAYSTAC, ADMX and ORGAN can also constrain dark photons after a polarization assumption is chosen, but their scan strategy and resonant response are different. Improvement factors should therefore be quoted only against the previous GigaBREAD fixed-polarization rescaling. The main remaining uncertainty is physical: structure formation may partially randomize a primordial vector direction, in which case neither the perfectly fixed nor perfectly isotropic limit is the complete description.

02Systematic Study of Cosmic Rays in Star-Forming and Starburst Galaxies using Fermi-LAT and Multiwavelength DataHinako Ochi, Tsunefumi Mizuno, Yasushi Fukazawa, Noemi Barnier, Susumu Inoue, Wataru Iwakiri, Junling Chen and Tomonori Totani

Fifteen years of Fermi data put starbursts at or below the cosmic-ray calorimetric limit

Paper: Systematic Study of Cosmic Rays in Star-Forming and Starburst Galaxies using Fermi-LAT and Multiwavelength Data — Hinako Ochi, Tsunefumi Mizuno, Yasushi Fukazawa, Noemi Barnier, Susumu Inoue, Wataru Iwakiri, Junling Chen and Tomonori Totani. arXiv:2611.07189v1, full text. Submitted 4 November 2026 at 22:25:01 UTC and first listed 6 November. The manuscript is accepted by the Publications of the Astronomical Society of Japan following peer review. Classification: observational gamma-ray analysis with conditional one-zone cosmic-ray modelling.

Question and data. Star-forming galaxies accelerate cosmic rays, whose hadronic interactions with gas produce gamma rays and neutrinos. A proton calorimeter loses nearly all of the relevant cosmic-ray power through collisions before escape. The authors ask how close ordinary star-forming galaxies and compact starbursts come to this limit, and whether their radio-to-TeV spectra require unusual electron-to-proton ratios.

The analysis uses 15 years of Fermi-LAT data and updated Galactic diffuse and point-source models for seven galaxies: M82, NGC 253, M31, Arp 220, NGC 2146, Arp 299 and M33. M31 is decomposed into central and southeast components. Spectra are fitted from 50 MeV to about 100 GeV, then combined with published radio and TeV measurements in NAIMA-based one-zone models.

Results. M82 and NGC 253 are detected with test statistics 1702 and 899 and show mild curvature. Their 0.1–100 GeV energy fluxes are 8.19×10−12 and 6.31×10−12 ergcm−2s−1. Both spectra turn down below a few hundred MeV, reducing the electron bremsstrahlung contribution relative to earlier models. The inferred electron-to-proton ratios are 1/25–1/30 at 1 GeV and 1/120–1/150 at 10 GeV, close to canonical Milky-Way values.

The proton energy densities are about 190 eV cm⁻³ in M82 and 470 eV cm⁻³ in NGC 253, respectively around eight and 2.5 times below the magnetic energy densities used in the fits. This argues against cosmic-ray–magnetic equipartition inside these one-zone starburst models.

For M31, a compact central component has a soft curved spectrum and a 95% upper limit of 0.33° on its radius. Unresolved sources can therefore contribute substantially, so its luminosity is treated as an upper limit on interstellar emission. M33 is similarly complicated by an offset toward the young region NGC 604.

Revised fluxes for NGC 2146 and Arp 220 are approximately a factor of two lower than in the 2020 comparison analysis. Across the starburst sample, the observed ratio of gamma-ray luminosity to the calorimetric prediction is 0.33–1.20; the value slightly above unity is within the combined modelling and source uncertainties, rather than proof of super-calorimetry. Ordinary star-forming galaxies lie around the 10% level, except for the SMC, where point sources are likely important.

Limitations. Calorimetric efficiency depends on infrared luminosity, gas density, source subtraction and the assumed supernova-to-cosmic-ray conversion. Several weak galaxies have TS~50, making their spectra sensitive to the diffuse foreground. M31 and M33 contain unresolved or localized components that are not clean measurements of diffuse cosmic-ray interactions. The multiwavelength fits assume one-zone geometry, adopted magnetic fields and published gas masses; the apparent departure from equipartition is consequently model-dependent. The work characterizes detected emission but does not directly measure the neutrino output or isolate hadronic photons event by event.

History

Fermi-LAT first reported M82 and NGC 253 at 6.8σ and 4.8σ with 11 months of data in 2010, establishing a connection between massive-star formation and GeV emission. A later uniform study using about ten years of LAT data detected 11 bona-fide galaxies and two candidates, plus a stacked below-threshold population, and estimated that star-forming galaxies contribute about 5% of the extragalactic gamma-ray background and 3% of the IceCube astrophysical-neutrino flux (Ajello et al., arXiv:2003.05493). Those population fractions are model estimates, not direct source associations.

The new analysis updates individual nearby systems rather than remeasuring the total background. Its most consequential changes are the sub-GeV downturn in M82 and NGC 253, the factor-of-two downward revisions for NGC 2146 and Arp 220, and explicit source-confusion cautions for M31 and M33. It supports the established picture that compact starbursts are efficient hadronic targets, while removing the need for several systems to sit clearly above the calorimetric relation. Remaining uncertainty is dominated by gas, magnetic-field and unresolved-source modelling, which also propagates into forecasts of the diffuse neutrino intensity.

03General-relativistic neutrino-radiation hydrodynamics of crust ejection in magnetar giant flaresTetyana Pitik, David Radice, Jacob Fields and Yi Qiu

General-relativistic simulations sharply reduce magnetar-flare r-process yields

Paper: General-relativistic neutrino-radiation hydrodynamics of crust ejection in magnetar giant flares — Tetyana Pitik, David Radice, Jacob Fields and Yi Qiu. arXiv:2611.07022v1, full text. Submitted 4 November 2026 at 19:00:06 UTC and first listed 6 November. v1 preprint; no journal publication is listed. Classification: one-dimensional numerical simulation and conditional nucleosynthesis forecast, not an observed yield.

Question and method. The radio afterglow of the 27 December 2004 giant flare from SGR 1806−20 implies at least about 10−9M⊙ of baryonic ejecta. Previous hydrodynamic and analytic calculations proposed that a magnetospheric fireball drives a shock into the neutron-star crust, ejecting neutron-rich matter that undergoes r-process nucleosynthesis. This paper tests that chain with general-relativistic hydrodynamics, a tabulated HS(DD2) nuclear equation of state, M1 neutrino transport and a SkyNet reaction network.

The simulations use a fixed spherical neutron-star spacetime and a one-kilometre, whole-surface pressure layer. Its peak pressure is labelled by the equivalent field B=1015–1016.5 G through PGF=B2/8π. Every model is repeated without neutrino interactions, and the layer width, position and shape are varied. These labels describe the imposed pressure; they are not measurements of an actual flare field.

Results. The models unbind approximately

10−10−5×10−8M⊙

of outer crust with mass-weighted asymptotic velocities 0.44–0.52 c. Only about 10−3 of the imposed fireball energy becomes ejecta kinetic energy. Neutrino cooling matters strongly only at the most extreme 1016.5-G pressure: it removes about a tenth of the layer energy while the shock is active and halves the ejecta mass. The ejecta retain Ye≃0.44–0.47.

The rapid, high-entropy expansion makes very few seed nuclei. Roughly 90% of the ejecta becomes alpha particles, while 7–10% remains as free neutrons. Even the strongest model produces at most 3.5×10−11M⊙ in nuclei with A≥90, three to four orders of magnitude below the earlier analytic calculation. Lanthanides and actinides remain below about 3×10−5 of the ejecta mass.

For the extreme model at 10 kpc, the authors forecast about two Super-Kamiokande and 15 Hyper-Kamiokande inverse-beta events, but this flare is far more energetic than the 2004 event. Flares with observed energies should yield no detectable neutrino count. Free-neutron decay instead powers a predicted ultraviolet/optical flash peaking after roughly 40–55 seconds; this is a model forecast, not an observed nova brevis.

Connection to the 2004 flare. Models with B=1015.5–1016 G eject enough mass at about 0.45 c to resemble the radio afterglow, but their whole-surface fireball energies are 60–600 times the roughly 3×1046 erg prompt spike. Scaling to that observed energy gives only (2–3.5)×10−11M⊙, 50–100 times less than the radio afterglow requires. The authors therefore conclude that a one-dimensional crust shock cannot by itself supply the observed baryons unless the flare released far more total energy or the true magnetic geometry couples energy much more efficiently.

Limitations. Spherical symmetry, instantaneous whole-surface heating and omission of magnetic forces probably overestimate ejection. Real flares are localized, laterally expanding and threaded by open and closed field lines. The deepest, marginally unbound shells dominate the already small heavy-element yield, leaving it uncertain by about a factor of two for A≥90 and more for lanthanides. The fixed spacetime, one-millisecond evolution and analytic continuation of trajectories add further approximations. Multidimensional general-relativistic magnetohydrodynamics is required before treating these values as physical rate predictions.

History

Cehula, Thompson and Metzger modelled the same shock-ejection mechanism with special-relativistic hydrodynamics and a simplified crust. For pressures around 1030–1031 ergcm−3, they obtained 1025–1026 g of ejecta at 0.3–0.6 c, sufficient for the 2004 radio afterglow, and estimated a Galactic r-process contribution near 10−9M⊙yr−1 (arXiv:2311.05681, MNRAS 528, 5323).

Patel et al. then applied an analytic ejecta profile and reaction network, predicting moderate third-peak production, a stronger light r-process and a 10–15 minute, 1039–1040 ergs−1 nova brevis for an SGR 1806−20-like ejecta mass (arXiv:2501.17253). A separate analysis interpreted delayed MeV emission from the 2004 event as decay of roughly 10−6M⊙ of fresh r-process material (arXiv:2501.09181).

The new calculation directly conflicts with that high-yield interpretation within its tested mechanism: its largest mass in nuclei heavier than alpha particles is about 8×10−11M⊙, four orders of magnitude below the delayed-MeV requirement. The discrepancy comes mainly from hydrodynamics: deep, cooler crust assumed to escape in the analytic profile falls back in the general-relativistic simulations. This does not disprove that the 2004 MeV component was real or radioactive; it shows that the proposed spherical crust-shock model cannot produce the inferred mass under the new transport and equation-of-state treatment. Geometry, magnetic stresses and an alternative baryon-loading channel remain open.

EP261105a gains a red optical candidate that may have faded within two hours

Einstein Probe/WXT triggered on EP261105a at 5 November 2026, 10:07:54 UTC. Its initial position was RA 91.013°, Dec −54.231°, with a 3-arcmin 90% radius. Automatic FXT follow-up found an uncatalogued X-ray source at RA 91.0035°, Dec −54.2664°, with a 20-arcsec 90% radius. The trigger circular did not provide a flux, spectrum, duration or false-alarm probability, so the event's physical class remains open. GCN 46155.

SVOM/VT began 0.72 hours after the trigger and found an uncatalogued candidate inside the FXT region at RA 91.00143°, Dec −54.26428°. At mid-times 0.85–0.87 hours it measured VT_R = 22.3 ± 0.2 AB and VT_B > 23.1 AB at 3σ, indicating a red source. GCN 46160. LCO difference imaging started 2.55 hours after trigger and did not recover the candidate to r > 23.0 AB at 3σ. GCN 46162. The two measurements are compatible with fading, but different filters, calibrations and the modest depth prevent a secure decay slope. MASTER observations around 12.9 hours reached only 19.4–19.5 mag, too shallow to add a useful constraint. GCN 46165.

The optical position is consistent with the X-ray localization, but one red detection followed by a limit does not yet establish a GRB afterglow, tidal-disruption event or other source class. No association significance or host redshift was available at the cutoff.

GRB 261104A: refined prompt spectrum, a tentative DECam source and an inconsistent X-ray-coordinate claim

SVOM/ECLAIRs refined GRB 261104A, triggered on 4 November at 20:36:44 UTC, to T90=20.1−3.8+3.3 s in 4–120 keV. A power law fitted from T0+7.6 to T0+27.5 s gives photon index −1.76 ± 0.19, fluence (1.38±0.43)×10−7 ergcm−2 and photon flux (2.55±0.45)×10−1 cm−2s−1; quoted errors are 68%. GCN 46163.

DECam difference imaging at 5.34 hours found AT 2026aigl at RA 6.336822°, Dec −54.412962°, measuring r = 23.35 ± 0.12 and i = 23.08 ± 0.13 AB. A faint archival point source lies within 0.16 arcsec and has a photometric redshift zph=1.04±0.19. No fading had been established, so the GRB association remained tentative. GCN 46164.

EP-FXT observed the field about 23 hours after trigger and detected several uncatalogued X-ray sources. Circular 46166 states that Source 1 is consistent with the optical candidate and gives a 0.5–10 keV flux (3.13±0.72)×10−13 ergcm−2s−1. However, its quoted position, RA 6.2972°, Dec −54.4603°, is about three arcminutes from the DECam coordinates, not consistent with them at the stated 10-arcsec error radius. GCN 46166. This may be a coordinate or identification error in the rapid circular; until corrected, the X-ray/optical association should not be claimed.

A smartwatch screens for hypertension without estimating blood pressure

Paolo Di Achille, Lawrence Cai and colleagues published Opportunistically Detecting Signs of Hypertension on a Consumer Smartwatch in Nature on 5 November 2026. The peer-reviewed study tests a screening classifier, not a replacement for a cuff and not a diagnosis engine.

The model combines photoplethysmography and accelerometer waveforms collected by a consumer smartwatch, without personal cuff calibration. Its prospective multicentre validation included 196 participants without known cardiovascular disease or antihypertensive treatment. Participants wore the device in ordinary life for about seven days, and the reference was 24-hour ambulatory blood-pressure monitoring, with hypertension defined primarily as mean systolic or diastolic pressure of at least 130/80 mmHg.

At the prespecified operating point, the watch classifier achieved 65.8% sensitivity (95% confidence interval 54.0–76.3%), 90.0% specificity (83.2–94.7%) and 80.6% positive predictive value (68.6–89.6%). Initial office screening in the same cohort had 55.3% sensitivity, 90.0% specificity and 77.8% positive predictive value. The model score correlated with 24-hour systolic pressure at r=0.66 and diastolic pressure at r=0.63. Sensitivity rose to 89.5% for the much higher 145/90-mmHg threshold, but the confidence interval was wide (66.9–98.7%).

An exploratory analysis used a separate PPG-only encoder on 76,268 UK Biobank participants without baseline hypertension or cardiovascular disease. A positive classification was associated with incident hypertension, adjusted hazard ratio 1.83 (95% confidence interval 1.77–1.90). That analysis used finger pulse-oximeter waveforms, a different model and observational outcomes; it does not independently validate the smartwatch classifier's screening accuracy.

The clinical distinction is essential. With 65.8% sensitivity, roughly one third of hypertensive participants were missed at the chosen threshold. The cohort was small and deliberately risk-enriched, while predictive value will change with prevalence in the deployed population. Performance across other watches, skin conditions, motion patterns, arrhythmias and treated patients remains uncertain. The system can prompt confirmatory cuff testing; it cannot report blood pressure, rule out hypertension or establish that notifications improve clinical outcomes.
