Science News

GRB 261108D gained a common near-infrared and optical counterpart candidate

SVOM/ECLAIRs triggered on GRB 261108D at 23:52:36 UTC on 8 November. PRIME began J-band observations about two hours later and found an uncatalogued source at RA 170.60452°, Dec −77.63159°, with J ≈ 18.8 AB, not corrected for Galactic extinction. A Legacy Survey object at the same position has a tentative photometric redshift of about 0.6, making it a possible host rather than a secure one. GCN 46210.

DECam observed the field on 9 November at 07:52:29 UTC, about eight hours after the trigger. Image subtraction against pre-burst templates detected a source only 0.3 arcsec from the PRIME position, at r = 22.13 ± 0.09 AB and i = 21.56 ± 0.11 AB. GCN 46211.

The positional agreement and detection in three bands make this the leading counterpart candidate. Association with the burst still requires temporal fading or spectroscopy; the underlying-galaxy redshift is photometric, and neither circular reports a burst redshift.

Einstein Probe localized a faint X-ray source inside the GRB 261108C error region

Einstein Probe's FXT observed GRB 261108C beginning 8 November at 22:10:33 UTC, 51 min 23 s after the SVOM/ECLAIRs trigger, for about 4,400 s. It detected a new X-ray source inside the earlier MXT error circle at RA 11.7271°, Dec −64.3027°, with a 10-arcsec 90% confidence radius. GCN 46212.

With the Galactic column fixed at NH=2.02×1020 cm−2, an absorbed power law gives photon index

Γ=2.8−1.0+1.1

and unabsorbed 0.5–10 keV flux

FX=(7.3−2.2+4.0)×10−14 ergcm−2s−1,

with the quoted uncertainties reported by the EP team. This is a plausible X-ray afterglow candidate and improves the position substantially. A single detection does not yet demonstrate fading or prove association. Earlier SVOM/VT observations found no credible optical source to 21.5 AB mag in the MXT region.

EP261105a remained detectable in X-rays but disappeared below a deep optical limit

Einstein Probe performed a second FXT observation of EP261105a beginning 6 November at 03:27:37 UTC, with 5,363 s exposure. The source remained at RA 91.0002°, Dec −54.2620°, with a 10-arcsec 90% confidence radius. With the Galactic column fixed at 4.84×1020 cm−2, the EP team obtained photon index 2.05−1.11+1.79 and unabsorbed 0.5–10 keV flux

FX=(5.31−2.75+6.48)×10−14 ergcm−2s−1

at 90% confidence. GCN 46204.

Gemini-South/GMOS observed the field on 7 November at 07:27:15 UTC, about 1.9 days after the WXT trigger, and did not detect the earlier optical candidate to r>24.6 AB at 3σ, without Galactic-extinction correction. GCN 46209. If the nearby galaxy with tentative photometric redshift z≈0.055 is the host, that limit corresponds to absolute magnitude fainter than about −12.4 AB.

Together with the previously reported decline from R≈22.3 at 0.85 h to R≈24.3 at 18 h, the Gemini result supports a fading transient. It does not by itself establish a kilonova or exclude all kilonova models: the host association and redshift remain tentative, and the comparison depends on extinction, filters and ejecta assumptions.

XMM-Newton detected persistent hard X-rays from the nearby Type Ia-CSM SN 2026sqf

An Astronomer's Telegram posted 9 November at 08:55 UTC reports an XMM-Newton detection of SN 2026sqf at about 102 days after explosion. The supernova lies in NGC 3310 at z=0.003312, corresponding to roughly 19 Mpc, and is the closest known Type Ia supernova with strong circumstellar interaction. ATel 18146, TNS AstroNote 2026-316.

XMM observed for 14 ks on 18 October. The preliminary measured 5–10 keV energy flux is approximately

F5−10keV≃1.5×10−13 ergcm−2s−1.

Adopting the same kT=10 keV thermal-bremsstrahlung spectrum and NH=2×1023 cm−2 used for the earlier Chandra analysis gives an extrapolated 0.3–10 keV flux of about 1.8×10−13 ergcm−2s−1, comparable to the Chandra measurement on 19 September. Persistence at this level supports continuing shock interaction with dense circumstellar material.

The XMM point-spread function admits contamination from nearby sources, so background refinement and the lower-energy signal remain under analysis. The detection is not a neutrino association: an earlier IceCube search around discovery found no excess, with p=1.00, and set a 90% confidence time-integrated E−2 muon-neutrino limit of E2dN/dE=7.4×10−2 GeVcm−2. ATel 17896.

Science Radar

Chiral self-sorting suppresses defects in end-linked polymer networks

Yuzhe Wang, Eduard O. Bobylev, Kang Wang, Heather J. Kulik and Jeremiah A. Johnson published Programming supramolecular polymer network topology via homochiral self-sorting in Nature Chemistry on 9 November 2026.

End-linked polymer networks often form primary loops, in which the two ends of one chain close on the same cross-link. Such loops consume linking sites without connecting different chains and weaken the macroscopic network. The authors terminate linear polymers with heterochiral bi-2-naphthol ligands and cross-link them through palladium(II)-mediated Pd2L4 metal–organic cages. Because the cages thermodynamically prefer homochiral ligand sets, a heterochiral pair of polymer ends is disfavoured from closing intramolecularly.

Compared with networks made from homochiral polymers, the heterochiral design shows lower primary-loop density and improved mechanical performance. At low concentrations, deliberately retained sacrificial loops can be opened or exchanged using functionalized free ligands, providing a route to edit network topology while preserving the bulk structure.

This is a materials-design demonstration in a specific palladium–ligand system. The abstract does not establish that the same energetic hierarchy will transfer to covalent networks, aqueous biomaterials or large-scale processing. Long-term creep, fatigue, metal recovery and the cost of the supramolecular components remain application-level questions.

Cryo-EM resolves the enzyme complex that completes chlorophyll synthesis

Dmitry Shvarev, Anna M. Wysocka, Roman Sobotka and colleagues published Structure of chlorophyll synthase in complex with the LHC-like protein HliD in Nature Chemical Biology on 9 November 2026.

Chlorophyll synthase, ChlG, completes chlorophyll-a biosynthesis by attaching a geranylgeranyl tail to chlorophyllide. In the cyanobacterium Synechocystis sp. PCC 6803, ChlG forms a complex with the high-light-inducible protein HliD. The authors resolved the ChlG2HliD2 complex by cryogenic electron microscopy at 3.0 Å without substrate and 3.2 Å with geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate bound.

The central HliD dimer binds two ChlG monomers and carries four chlorophylls, two zeaxanthins and two myxoxanthophylls. Combined with site-directed mutagenesis, molecular-dynamics simulations and structural modelling, the maps identify the geranylgeranyl-pyrophosphate pocket and support a mechanism for substrate recognition and esterification. The pigment arrangement in HliD closely resembles features of plant and algal light-harvesting complexes, strengthening the proposed evolutionary connection between cyanobacterial stress proteins and eukaryotic antenna proteins.

The work resolves a catalytic scaffold and supports a mechanism; it does not directly image every catalytic intermediate or chlorophyll transfer into a photosystem. Chlorophyllide placement was structurally modelled rather than captured in the cryo-EM maps, and the complex comes from one cyanobacterial species. How stress-dependent partner exchange controls pigment delivery in living cells remains open.
