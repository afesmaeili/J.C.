Astrophysical Alerts

GRB 261110A continued to fade, strengthening its optical-afterglow identification

SVOM/ECLAIRs triggered on GRB 261110A at 11:50:00 UTC on 10 November 2026. Follow-up reported after the previous edition confirms that the candidate at RA 350.8530709°, Dec +33.0630451° continued to fade. CrAO/ZTSh measured R = 22.30 ± 0.10 at 6.12 h and R = 22.80 ± 0.09 at 10.81 h after the trigger (Vega magnitudes); SVOM/COLIBRÍ then measured r = 23.60 ± 0.19 AB between 14.57 and 16.88 h. GCN 46228, GCN 46223.

MITSuME observations beginning 8.7 min after the trigger did not detect the source, with the deepest stacked limits g′ > 20.2, Rc > 20.3 and Ic > 19.3 AB. Those limits are shallower than the later detections and therefore do not contradict the afterglow interpretation. GCN 46222. The positional agreement and multi-epoch fading now make this a well-supported optical afterglow, but no redshift or spectroscopic classification had been reported by the cutoff. Because the measurements use different filters and magnitude systems and are not corrected for Galactic extinction, they should not be combined into a precision decay slope without a consistent reanalysis.

Fermi/GBM detected the likely long GRB 261111A; no counterpart was reported

Fermi/GBM triggered on GRB 261111A at 11:27:07 UTC on 11 November 2026 and classified it as a likely long GRB. The final real-time localization was RA 56.9°, Dec +5.8°, with a 2.0° statistical uncertainty; the distributed HEALPix map includes the estimated systematic component. GCN 46224. An independent BALROG fit gave RA 54.1°, Dec +6.1°, a 3.2° 1σ statistical radius, and an estimated additional 1° systematic error. GCN 46225.

MASTER-SAAO began imaging about 6.7 h after the trigger and reported no new optical source, with individual/coadded limits reaching approximately 18.9 mag over portions of the broad GBM region. GCN 46229. This is a gamma-ray detection with a degree-scale localization, not a confirmed multiwavelength or multimessenger association; the optical non-detection is coverage- and depth-limited.

Einstein Probe found the uncatalogued X-ray transient EP261111a

Einstein Probe/WXT triggered on EP261111a at 13:25:10 UTC on 11 November 2026. The WXT position is RA 43.118°, Dec +44.444°, with a 3-arcmin radius at 90% confidence, including statistical and systematic uncertainty. An automatic FXT follow-up found an uncatalogued X-ray source at RA 43.1202°, Dec +44.4365°, with a 20-arcsec 90% confidence radius. GCN 46226.

Xinglong began optical imaging 0.391 h after the trigger and found no candidate within the WXT region to g > 20.955 AB at 5σ. GCN 46227. MASTER-SAAO observed roughly 7.1 h after trigger with shallower unfiltered limits of about 17.8 mag. GCN 46230. No X-ray flux, temporal classification, optical counterpart or redshift was available at the cutoff, so EP261111a remains an unclassified X-ray transient; the optical limits do not establish the absence of a counterpart.

A host redshift narrows—but does not settle—the interpretation of EP261105a

An ePESSTO+ spectrum obtained with NTT/EFOSC2 beginning 06:51 UTC on 11 November 2026 measures z = 0.0765 ± 0.0002 for the proposed host galaxy of EP261105a, from Hα, Hβ, [O II], [O III] and [N II] emission lines. In the cosmology adopted by the observers, this corresponds to a luminosity distance of 347 Mpc. If the galaxy is the true host, the previously reported R ≈ 24.3 counterpart at 18 h has M_R ≈ −13.6 after Milky Way extinction correction. GCN 46231.

That luminosity is at the faint end of kilonova models, but the existing optical data are also consistent with a power-law decay. The spectroscopy secures the galaxy redshift, not the transient association: a higher-redshift transient in chance projection remains possible. The report therefore does not constitute a kilonova identification.

ASKAP and Murriyang confirmed the new repeater FRB 20261031B

ASKAP/CRACO detected two bursts from FRB 20261031B at 10:56:18.023 and 12:00:29.265 UTC on 31 October 2026, separated by about one hour. They had signal-to-noise ratios 14 and 11 at a central frequency of 943 MHz and a common dispersion measure of 581.4 pc cm⁻³. Voltage capture localized the source to sub-arcsecond precision at RA 01:15:25.41(6), Dec −80:39:18.6(6). Murriyang/Parkes detected a third burst on 5 November at 10:07:46.714 UTC, with S/N = 61.4 and rotation measure 22.1 ± 0.1 rad m⁻², confirming continued repetition. The discovery was reported on 11 November at 19:14 UTC. ATel 18153.

A putative host has r = 23.2 and photometric redshift z = 0.6 ± 0.1 (68% interval). If that identification and photometric redshift are confirmed, the source could be the highest-redshift known repeater. Both conditions remain provisional: spectroscopy and a quantified association probability are needed before treating the redshift or record claim as established.

EP trigger 01709322452 was classified as a stellar flare

Einstein Probe/WXT triggered at 00:17:37 UTC on 12 November 2026. An initial MASTER follow-up began 397 s after the trigger and found no new optical source to approximately 19.0 mag. GCN 46232. The EP team subsequently associated the X-ray event with PM J04414+1404, classifying it as a likely stellar flare with estimated 0.5–4.0 keV flux ≈ 6 × 10⁻¹¹ erg s⁻¹ cm⁻² and luminosity ≈ 1.1 × 10³¹ erg s⁻¹. GCN 46233. This revised classification removes the basis for treating the trigger as an unidentified extragalactic transient.

Science News

Hideki Shirakawa (1936–2026): the chemist who helped make polymers conduct electricity

Nature published a scientific obituary on 11 November for Hideki Shirakawa, who died on 24 August 2026, aged 90; his death was announced by the Institute of Science Tokyo on 3 September. The date distinction matters: this is a new appraisal of his scientific legacy, not a newly reported death.

Shirakawa developed a method for making coherent polyacetylene films and, with Alan MacDiarmid and Alan Heeger, showed in 1977 that halogen doping could raise their conductivity by about nine orders of magnitude, reaching roughly 10^5 S m^−1. That result overturned the assumption that plastics were necessarily electrical insulators and established conducting polymers as a field spanning polymer chemistry and condensed-matter physics. The three shared the 2000 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the discovery and development of conducting polymers. The broader legacy includes organic light-emitting devices, photovoltaics, sensors and flexible electronics, although the original doped polyacetylene itself was chemically fragile and did not become the dominant commercial material.

AlphaGenome 2 resolves regulatory-variant effects by cell state, but remains a predictive preprint

Google DeepMind released AlphaGenome 2 and its preprint on 11 November. The model takes a 1-megabase DNA sequence plus a tissue, cell type or cell-state condition and predicts gene expression, chromatin accessibility, methylation, contacts, splicing and other molecular readouts. Its cell encoder was trained on more than 100 million single-cell RNA profiles from 68 tissues; the accompanying resources score about 7 million variants across 510 conditions and provide enhancer–gene predictions across 640 conditions.

On held-out TenK10K immune-cell benchmarks, AlphaGenome 2 recovered 30% of 6,346 fine-mapped single-cell eQTLs at 90% precision, compared with 16% for a single-cell-fine-tuned AlphaGenome 1 baseline; for GTEx bulk-tissue eQTLs, recall at 90% precision rose from 17% to 26%. These are retrospective benchmark results from the developers, not experimental discoveries or clinical validation. The authors report that high-precision recall still captures only a minority of causal variants, performance is weaker for inaccessible chromatin, very distal and post-transcriptional mechanisms, and transient, disease and fine neuronal states are underrepresented. Condition embeddings are learned from observational profiles, so they do not establish causal regulatory responses. A 9 November independent preprint found that AlphaGenome 1 often failed to propagate simulated promoter disruption into downstream RNA-seq predictions; that test does not directly evaluate AlphaGenome 2, but it shows why orthogonal perturbation experiments remain essential.

Correcting one UNC13A splice defect restores synaptic function in cellular ALS models

A peer-reviewed, open-access Nature paper published 11 November tested whether loss of nuclear TDP-43 disrupts synaptic signalling through cryptic splicing of UNC13A, a presynaptic vesicle-priming gene. The authors used human iPSC-derived cortical-like neurons and spinal motor neurons, induced TDP-43 loss, deleted the cryptic exon genetically, and developed splice-switching antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs). They also tested splice correction in a mouse engineered with the human UNC13A sequence.

Restoring about 30% or more of normal UNC13A protein was sufficient to recover spontaneous and evoked synaptic transmission in the cell models. The principal electrophysiology comparisons included 19 versus 19 neurons for one ASO/control analysis, 34 control, 30 TDP-43-depleted and 25 ASO-treated spinal motor neurons, and four independent multielectrode-array experiments for network synchrony. Single-synapse release probability moved toward rescue but narrowly missed conventional significance (P = 0.052). The mouse experiment established in-vivo splice correction, not improved survival or behaviour. The cultures had homogeneous TDP-43 loss and consisted only of excitatory neurons, whereas human ALS is mosaic and circuit-level effects include inhibitory neurons. This is therefore strong mechanistic and preclinical evidence, not evidence of safety or efficacy in patients.

First human safety study of an oral actinide chelator

A Phase 1, non-randomized trial published in JAMA on 11 November evaluated HOPO 14-1, an oral formulation of 3,4,3-LI(1,2-HOPO) intended to bind internally deposited actinides and some heavy metals. Forty-two healthy adults, six at each of seven dose levels from 100 to 7,500 mg, received one dose and were followed for 14 days. Recruitment ran from March 2023 to July 2025, with follow-up completed in August 2025 (NCT05628961).

No serious adverse events or dose-limiting toxicities occurred. Drug-related events were diarrhea in 31%, vomiting in 14%, headache in 12% and fatigue in 7%, all mild or moderate; gastrointestinal effects were most evident at the highest doses. Median time to peak plasma concentration ranged from 0.86 to 1.90 hours, exposure increased with dose, and elimination was mainly fecal with little urinary excretion. The study establishes single-dose pharmacokinetics and short-term tolerability only. It was open-label, single-centre and had no control group; participants were healthy and uncontaminated, so it did not measure actinide removal, repeated-dose safety or clinical benefit after radioactive exposure.

A 3.95-billion-year-old gneiss points to transient subduction on the early Earth

A peer-reviewed Nature study published 11 November reports trondhjemitic gneiss from eastern Hebei, China, dated to 3.95 billion years. Magmatic zircons have mean δ^18O = +6.77 ± 0.23‰ and weighted mean εHf(t) = +1.45 ± 0.27. Together with whole-rock trace elements, thermodynamic modelling favours 8–16% partial melting of low-temperature-altered basaltic crust at pressures above 1.6 GPa and 740–820 °C—conditions corresponding to burial beyond roughly 50 km.

The authors interpret the cool, rapid burial as a transient subduction event and infer that the basaltic source came from mantle depleted during the Hadean. This adds a rare >3.9-Gyr rock record outside Canada's Acasta complex and supports localized recycling of surface-altered crust by 3.95 Ga. The conclusion is model-dependent and rests on a very sparse surviving rock record; evidence for one transient high-pressure event does not demonstrate globally continuous, modern-style plate tectonics at that time.

Halogen-free aqueous electrolyte enables four-electron iodine cycling

A peer-reviewed, open-access Nature paper published 11 November addresses a central obstacle in aqueous metal–iodine batteries: stabilizing reactive I⁺ intermediates without concentrated, corrosive halide electrolytes. The authors combine a kosmotropic anion, a polar anion and a non-halogen nucleophile. In their mechanism, the polar anion creates a water-poor, anion-rich electrode interface while the kosmotropic anion weakens nucleophile–water interactions, allowing Nuc–I⁺–Nuc halogen bonds to stabilize the four-electron I⁻/I⁰/I⁺ pathway.

The framework worked with four nucleophiles and in Zn–I₂ and Al–I₂ laboratory cells. Zn–I₂ cells retained 96% capacity after 1,000 cycles at 1 A g⁻¹ and 94% after 2,000 cycles at 2 A g⁻¹, and four-electron conversion persisted up to 12.2 mg cm⁻² iodine loading. Spectroscopy, micro-CT, molecular simulations and electrochemistry support the proposed interfacial mechanism. Practical readiness remains unproven: most measurements used coin cells, the economic advantage is modelled, and the authors identify incomplete Coulombic efficiency and irreversible iodine-inventory loss that worsens at higher loading. Long-duration pouch-cell validation, safety and system-level energy metrics are still needed.
