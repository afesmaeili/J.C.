#### 1. S106: gamma rays do not uniquely identify a proton accelerator

**“HAWC, Fermi-LAT and Chandra Detections of High Energy Particles in the S106 Star Forming Region” — Rishi Babu, Stephen DiKerby, Tulun Ergin, Shuo Zhang, Mehr Un Nisa and the HAWC Collaboration** (arXiv author listing; the PDF gives the full collaboration roster). [arXiv:2610.41163v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41163v1), [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.41163v1). Submitted **30 October 2026, 02:38:48 UTC**; listed **2 November**; primary category **astro-ph.HE**. **Observational analysis plus competing emission models; preprint.**

Can a star-forming region's very-high-energy gamma rays be produced by electrons, or do they require accelerated protons and accompanying neutrinos? The authors combine approximately **2,900 days of HAWC data**, Fermi-LAT observations from **4 August 2008 to 20 July 2024**, and a **45 ks archival Chandra exposure** of Sharpless 106, at an adopted distance of **1.4 kpc**. They fit neighboring sources and Galactic diffuse emission, then model the broadband spectrum with synchrotron radiation, bremsstrahlung, inverse-Compton scattering and, alternatively, pion decay.

The HAWC fit using a Planck dust spatial template gives **TS = 31** and photon index **2.61 ± 0.11 statistical, with systematic uncertainty +0.21/−0.19**. The fitted differential flux at **7 TeV** is **4.8 (+1.1/−0.9 statistical; +2.3/−1.4 systematic) × 10⁻¹⁵ TeV⁻¹ cm⁻² s⁻¹**. These are the reported fit errors, not a separately calibrated confidence interval for source association. The Fermi analysis favors an extended source, with Gaussian radius **0.39° ± 0.07°** and a **200 MeV–1 TeV** photon index **2.18 ± 0.06**.

The important modeling ingredient is S106's own infrared radiation:

\[
u_{\rm IR}(r)=\frac{L_{\rm dust}}{4\pi r^2c}
\simeq60\left(\frac{r}{\mathrm{pc}}\right)^{-2}\mathrm{eV\,cm^{-3}}.
\]

With gas density fixed at **250 cm⁻³**, one electron population can reproduce the broadband spectrum. Its representative fit has **B = 2.38 μG**, electron cutoff **1,753 TeV**, electron energy above 1 GeV **4.11 × 10⁴⁵ erg**, and characteristic inverse-Compton distance **3.55 pc**. These correlated model values are **not independent measurements or confidence limits**. Figure 7 demonstrates spectral compatibility, not a unique identification of the particles.

A hybrid model also works: its magnetic field is about **18.2 μG**, with a proton component carrying about **3.1 × 10⁴⁶ erg above 1 GeV**. The proton cutoff reaches the imposed **1,000 TeV upper boundary**. A boundary-hitting fit is not a measured cutoff, and does not by itself establish a proton PeVatron.

The diffuse-background treatment matters: using the unmasked diffuse template gives a reported **4.8σ post-trial** result, whereas the adopted analysis masks the dense S106 cloud out of that background and fits its contribution separately. The electron-only fit also uses a field below the **10–500 μG** range cited for the core; different emission zones may explain this, but that spatial interpretation is not independently established. Unresolved stellar X-rays can contaminate the nominal diffuse component, and the Chandra snapshot is not contemporaneous with the long gamma-ray integrations. No neutrino detection from S106 is reported.

##### History

**Giardino, Favata and Micela (2004)**, [astro-ph/0406344](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0406344), already analyzed the **3 November 2001 Chandra observation**, identifying **87 X-ray sources** within 4.4 arcmin and examining the central young massive object. The new paper reuses this exposure but masks resolved sources to investigate diffuse emission. Its contribution is the broadband connection to GeV–TeV emission, rather than discovery of S106's X-ray activity. The earlier source census makes unresolved-source contamination a concrete concern.

**Aharonian, Yang and de Oña Wilhelmi (2019)**, [arXiv:1804.02331v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.02331v2), argued for hadronic emission around massive clusters using spatially resolved gamma rays and gas maps. Their approximately **1/r cosmic-ray density profiles** supported sustained injection; cooling and the absence of a central inverse-Compton brightening disfavored electrons for those extended systems. Their argument was morphological and source-specific, not a theorem that every star-forming region must be hadronic.

S106 therefore does not overturn the cluster evidence. It illustrates why a compact region with a strong local infrared field needs its own transport and radiation model. The decisive unresolved issue is whether a physically consistent spatial model can reproduce the X-ray, gas and gamma-ray morphologies together. The current spectral degeneracy leaves both electron-dominated and hybrid interpretations open.

#### 2. “Missing mergers”: a long-delay claim driven by one old host

**“Missing Mergers: On the Unexpected Delay-Time Distribution of Binary Neutron Star Mergers” — Ryan J. Foley.** [arXiv:2610.40782v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40782v1), [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.40782v1). Submitted **29 October 2026, 18:00:10 UTC**; listed **2 November**; primary category **astro-ph.HE**. **Population inference under restrictive assumptions; preprint.**

The delay-time distribution, or DTD, describes the interval between star formation and a binary-neutron-star merger. For gravitational-wave inspiral, the strong dependence of merger time on orbital separation motivates a broad distribution close to **t⁻¹**. GW170817's host, NGC 4993, instead presents a particularly old stellar population.

Foley fits ultraviolet-to-infrared photometry and an integrated VLT/MUSE spectrum with a 20-bin, nonparametric star-formation history. The nominal model uses a Chabrier initial mass function, MIST stellar tracks, MILES spectra and a continuity prior between adjacent star-formation bins, while fitting metallicity, dust and nuisance parameters. It yields a **mass-weighted stellar age of 12.4 (+0.5/−0.7) Gyr, at 90% posterior credibility**. This is a host-population inference, not a direct clock reading for the merging binary.

The population model is

\[
\mathrm{DTD}(t)=A t^\alpha,\qquad
 t_{\min}\leq t\leq13.68\;\mathrm{Gyr}.
\]

It combines the host history with a cosmic star-formation history and two GW-detected BNS events, GW170817 and GW190425. Only the first supplies host-age information. The nominal priors are uniform in **−10 < α < 0** and **0.01 < t_min < 13.45 Gyr**; the maximum age is fixed. Metallicity-dependent binary formation is ignored.

Under the paper's **formation-efficiency normalization treatment**, marginalizing the normalization and slope gives **t_min > 7.5 Gyr at 90% credibility** and **>5.6 Gyr at 95%**, with approximately **0.2 Gyr run-to-run scatter** in each threshold. That scatter is not an additional observational confidence interval. A fixed model with **(t_min, α) = (12.6 Gyr, −1)** has a normalization-marginalized likelihood **40.6 ± 9.4** times that of **(10 Myr, −1)** in the efficiency treatment, or **138 ± 32** in the rate treatment. These are comparisons of specified DTD shapes, not Bayes factors integrated over their shape parameters or probabilities that a formation channel is correct.

A crucial limitation appears in the likelihood itself. In the rate-normalized treatment, the Poisson count, surveyed volume-time and rate prior factor out of the DTD-shape inference. Its remaining dependence is proportional to

\[
\frac{\int_{t_{\min}}^{t_{\rm univ}}
\mathrm{SFH}_{4993}(t)t^\alpha\,dt}
{\int_{t_{\min}}^{t_{\rm univ}}
\psi_{\rm cosmic}(t)t^\alpha\,dt}.
\]

The posterior then accumulates at its upper prior boundary. Changing to the efficiency treatment changes the implied shape prior and produces the quoted finite bounds. Thus the result is driven chiefly by **one host relative to cosmic star formation**, rather than by several independently dated mergers. The stellar fit also leaves abundance-ratio systematics unmodeled, and the paper acknowledges that a mixture of formation channels is more plausible than its single truncated power law. “Missing mergers” is a model-dependent tension, not evidence that LVK overlooked a measured population.

##### History

**Zevin et al. (2022)**, [arXiv:2206.02814v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02814v2), inferred a DTD from **68 short-GRB hosts**, obtaining **α = −1.83 (+0.35/−0.39)** and **t_min = 184 (+67/−79) Myr**, both 90% credible intervals. They marginalized host-history and DTD uncertainties, fixed the binary formation efficiency, and assumed detection selection did not depend on DTD parameters. Their minimum-delay prior covered **5–500 Myr**. Faint hosts, large offsets and afterglow selection were explicit limitations. Those results concern a selected GRB population and cannot be translated directly into a universal BNS bound.

An independent, more recent comparison is **Pracchia and Salafia**, [arXiv:2601.03861v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03861v2), revised **5 August 2026**. Their hierarchical population analysis uses **210 flux-selected Fermi short bursts** and a joint Fermi/Swift sample of **18 bursts, 16 with redshifts**, explicitly modeling detection and redshift-selection cuts. Both empirical luminosity-function and structured-jet treatments favor short delays. Their power-law models give mean-delay medians of **77 Myr** and **105 Myr**, respectively, with broad 90% intervals extending to hundreds of Myr. These are population means, not minimum-delay bounds, and their slope convention has the opposite sign to Foley's.

The new paper identifies a potentially important conflict between an old nearby GW host and selected cosmological GRBs. It does not yet resolve that conflict: host-age modeling, selection, neutron-star–black-hole contamination of the GRB sample, and multiple BNS channels remain alternatives. The new conditional lower bound is numerically much larger than earlier estimates, but calling that an “improvement” would be misleading because the populations, priors and likelihoods differ.

#### 1. EP261030a: a fast X-ray transient with a later X-ray localization

The transient began on **30 October at 17:15:19 UTC**, lasting about **100 s** in Einstein Probe/WXT. The edited **31 October** report gives a **3.2 arcmin radius at 90% confidence** and a **0.5–4 keV unabsorbed flux of 3.6 (+1.1/−0.9) × 10⁻¹⁰ erg cm⁻² s⁻¹**, from an absorbed power law with Galactic column fixed at **2.4 × 10²⁰ cm⁻²**. [GCN 46121, v2](https://gcn.nasa.gov/circulars/46121).

FXT observed on **31 October at 23:08:47 UTC**, about 30 hours later, finding an X-ray source inside the WXT region at **RA 23.6760°, Dec −11.6749°**, with **10 arcsec radius at 90% confidence**, including systematics. Its **0.5–10 keV flux was 1.6 (+2.2/−0.9) × 10⁻¹³ erg cm⁻² s⁻¹**, with 90% errors and the same fixed Galactic column. Different bands and spectral uncertainties prevent treating the two quoted fluxes as a precise fading factor. [GCN 46125, 1 November](https://gcn.nasa.gov/circulars/46125).

GROWTH-India found no optical counterpart down to **r′ > 21.28 mag, 5σ**, at **21.493 hours** after the trigger; the limit is not corrected for Galactic extinction. The report's reference to an FXT position points to the earlier WXT circular, so its positional wording should be treated cautiously. The source class remains unresolved; an optical non-detection does not establish the absence of an afterglow. No event false-alarm rate is supplied in these circulars. [GCN 46124](https://gcn.nasa.gov/circulars/46124).

#### 2. MAXI J1609−487: evidence shifts away from the GRB interpretation

**ATel 18136, issued 1 November at 04:23 UTC**, reports renewed activity of the source also labeled **GRB 261028B**. Its **4–10 keV flux was 33 ± 6 mCrab** from **30 October 05:43 to 31 October 00:18 UTC**, followed by an increase. The team warns that the increase is affected by incomplete background and effective-area corrections near the detector edge.

The **28 October flare** had an absorbed power-law photon index **1.79 (+0.43/−0.37)** and column **2.3 (+3.7/−2.3) × 10²² cm⁻²**, with **90% errors**, and average **2–10 keV flux 8.5 × 10⁻⁹ erg cm⁻² s⁻¹**. Recurrence, spectrum and low Galactic latitude favor a **Galactic transient**, possibly a supergiant fast X-ray transient. The latter is a proposed classification, not a confirmed binary identification. [Primary telegram](https://www.astronomerstelegram.org/?read=18136).

#### 3. FRB 20261029A: a radio burst, but no secure host galaxy

**GREENBURST announced the event on 30 October at 21:46 UTC**; the observation occurred on **29 October at 10:50:08 UTC**. The Green Bank Telescope detected it over **960–1,920 MHz**, with **signal-to-noise 8.3**, approximately **8 ms width**, and dispersion measure **456.7 ± 0.1 pc cm⁻³**. The nominal pointing is **RA 04h28m28s, Dec +26°32′31″**, with roughly **9 arcmin uncertainty**.

The dispersion substantially exceeds the cited Galactic model contribution. However, a galaxy **4.6 arcmin** from the pointing is only a positional candidate. Its catalogued spectroscopic and photometric redshifts disagree, and the spectrum has a quality concern. Neither the broad localization nor a plausible dispersion–redshift relation establishes an association. No secure redshift or host is available from this announcement; signal-to-noise is not a published false-alarm probability. [ATel 18131](https://www.astronomerstelegram.org/?read=18131).

#### 4. GRB 261029A follow-up: a candidate soft thermal spectral component

A substantive update to Friday's burst coverage came from **SVOM/ECLAIRs on 30 October at 21:20 UTC**. For its trigger at **29 October 01:27:07 UTC**, ECLAIRs measures **T₉₀ = 47.5 (+1.0/−0.9) s in 4–120 keV**. A power law plus blackbody fits the time-averaged **5–120 keV** spectrum with **χ²/dof = 40/26**, compared with **83.8/28** for a simple power law. The fitted blackbody temperature is **1.42 ± 0.17 keV**, with quoted spectral errors at **68% confidence**.

This motivates testing a soft thermal component. It is not yet a unique photospheric interpretation: alternative spectral shapes, calibration and time evolution require assessment, and the circular supplies no calibrated component-detection significance. The refined **2.31 arcmin, 90%** localization contains the known optical afterglow. [GCN 46117](https://gcn.nasa.gov/circulars/46117).

#### Chemical imaging of living cells reaches 19.2 three-dimensional volumes per second

**Masato Fukushima, Keiichiro Toda, Yusei Sugawara, Shotaro Kawano and Takuro Ideguchi**, “Video-rate volumetric chemical imaging via mid-infrared photothermal optical diffraction tomography,” published **30 October 2026** in *Nature Communications*, **17, 11075**. [Research article and full text](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-78699-z); [dated university announcement](https://www.u-tokyo.ac.jp/focus/en/press/z0508_00481.html).

Mid-infrared absorption heats molecular bonds slightly; visible-light measurements detect the resulting refractive-index change. Rapid scanning over **11 illumination angles** and differential tomographic reconstruction produce label-free chemical volumes at **19.2 volumes s⁻¹**, about **52 ms per volume**, without temporal averaging. A separate multi-wavenumber mode covers **300 cm⁻¹ in 1 s**—the fastest volumetric rate should not be confused with a complete spectrum at every time point.

The biological demonstration tracks **50 lipid-droplet trajectories** and distinguishes motion that two-dimensional projection obscures. This is a peer-reviewed imaging-method result, not a disease-treatment result. The live-cell experiment used **three cells from one dish**; larger replication applies to other imaging tests. Demonstrated specimens are only several to about ten micrometers thick. Water absorption, scattering and the weak-scattering reconstruction assumption limit extension to deep tissue. The advance is fast three-dimensional chemical dynamics at the single-cell scale; broad biological generalization remains to be established.
