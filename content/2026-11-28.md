### Astrophysical Alerts

#### IceCube-261123A — spectroscopy favors a Type I supernova in the neutrino region

On **26 November 2026 at 21:42 UTC**, the Telescopio Nazionale Galileo obtained a spectrum of **AT 2026ahvn** (ZTF26accqpcr). ZTF had detected the optical transient as early as **20 October**. It lies inside the 90% localization of the **23 November 06:54:16.99 UTC** IceCube Bronze track candidate (energy estimate **145 TeV**, signalness **0.457**, atmospheric false-alarm rate **1.205 yr⁻¹**). [IceCube notice](https://gcn.gsfc.nasa.gov/notices_amon_g_b/143530_34667334.amon) · [ZTF follow-up](https://gcn.nasa.gov/circulars/46370)

GRAWITA reported the spectrum on **27 November at 15:32:58 UTC**. Features **10,000–20,000 km s⁻¹** wide at half maximum match a Type I supernova roughly 15–20 days past peak. A Type Ia template at **z = 0.075** and a broad-lined Type Ic template at **z = 0.10** both fit. The host galaxy and redshift are uncertain. The spectrum showed no narrow emission lines that would support the proposed interaction with circumstellar material. The spectroscopy narrows the optical classification while leaving the neutrino association unconfirmed. [GRAWITA spectrum and classification](https://gcn.nasa.gov/circulars/46386)

#### GRB 261126A — SVOM confirms the prompt source and refines the candidate afterglow position

On **27 November at 12:06:44 UTC**, SVOM/ECLAIRs reported that imaging with the full event data confirms a point source despite the changing background in the initial alert. Emission began on **26 November at 19:03:20 UTC**, five seconds before the onboard trigger. The source produced two peaks, about **14 s** and **8 s** long, across **44 s** in 4–20 keV. The respective 4–120 keV fluences were \((4.08\pm0.58)\times10^{-7}\) and \((2.05\pm0.53)\times10^{-7}\) erg cm⁻² (68% uncertainties). [Initial alert](https://gcn.nasa.gov/circulars/46373) · [ECLAIRs analysis](https://gcn.nasa.gov/circulars/46382)

MXT began imaging on **26 November at 19:05:44 UTC**. Its **27 November 13:11:07 UTC** report used the first 450 s to localize the fading candidate afterglow at **RA 01h49m11s, Dec +09°44′38″** (J2000), with an **89″ radius at 90% confidence**, superseding the onboard 145″ radius. The count rate declined as \(t^{-1.36\pm0.20}\) and fell below the MXT detection limit after 800 s from the start of the pointing. [MXT localization and decay](https://gcn.nasa.gov/circulars/46384)

SVOM/VT started imaging **217 s after the trigger**. It reported **5σ** limits of **VT_B > 22.7** at +15.2 min and **VT_R > 22.7** at +14.7 min, followed by **VT_B > 23.0** at +4.09 h and **VT_R > 22.5** at +4.12 h. The **28 November 04:39:34 UTC** report found no credible new optical source in the refined MXT region. H.E.S.S. lists its planned pointing as cancelled because of the delay, so it provides no very-high-energy flux constraint. [VT follow-up](https://gcn.nasa.gov/circulars/46388) · [H.E.S.S. observing log](https://hess-experiment.eu/science/hess-grb-obs/)

#### EP261126a — the X-ray spectrum softens and the refined position lies farther from the proposed star

Einstein Probe’s **28 November 03:43:17 UTC** analysis traced the **26 November** onset to **21:55:53 UTC**, before the 22:04:27 WXT trigger. WXT recorded weak emission followed by a bright pulse across about **500 s** before the slew interrupted the observation. Its 0.5–4 keV photon index was **0.85 ± 0.35**, with mean unabsorbed flux \(4.13^{+1.16}_{-0.94}\times10^{-10}\) erg cm⁻² s⁻¹. FXT began at **22:07:59 UTC** and measured a softer 0.5–10 keV spectrum with photon index **2.46 ± 0.18** and flux \(1.73^{+0.21}_{-0.19}\times10^{-12}\) erg cm⁻² s⁻¹. The errors are at 90% confidence. The two fluxes cover different energy bands. [Einstein Probe analysis](https://gcn.nasa.gov/circulars/46387)

The revised FXT position is **RA 35.5409°, Dec −0.6357°** (J2000), with a **10″** 90% error radius, down from 20″. Using the UCAC4 catalogue position and proper motion, **UCAC4 447-002984** lies approximately **32″** from the revised centroid at the observation epoch. The original circular quoted 26″ from the preliminary centroid. The new position weakens the proposed identification with that star. The transient’s origin remains open. [Original localization](https://gcn.nasa.gov/circulars/46378) · [UCAC4 catalogue entry](https://vizier.cfa.harvard.edu/viz-bin/asu-tsv?-source=I%2F322A&UCAC4=447-002984&-out=UCAC4%2CRAJ2000%2CDEJ2000%2CpmRA%2CpmDE%2Ce_pmRA%2Ce_pmDE&-out.max=3)

#### GRB 261122B — Konus-Wind traces three emission episodes to about 1 MeV

Konus-Wind triggered on **22 November at 22:46:55.695 UTC**. Its **27 November 13:24:46 UTC** report describes three groups of peaks separated by quiet intervals across about **204 s**, with emission reaching approximately **1 MeV**. In **20 keV–10 MeV**, the total fluence was \(9.03^{+2.41}_{-1.61}\times10^{-6}\) erg cm⁻². The peak energy flux in a 64 ms interval beginning at +0.174 s was \(3.93^{+3.03}_{-2.11}\times10^{-6}\) erg cm⁻² s⁻¹. The power-law index was harder near maximum, \(-1.62^{+0.22}_{-0.26}\) over the first 0.256 s, than over the full 205.056 s fit, \(-2.56^{+0.27}_{-0.33}\). These preliminary uncertainties are at 68% confidence. [Konus-Wind measurements](https://gcn.nasa.gov/circulars/46385)

#### GRB 261119A — SVOM independently resolves the subsecond pulse

SVOM/GRM triggered on **19 November at 19:01:35.10 UTC**. Its **27 November 12:41:43 UTC** report measured \(T_{90}=0.35^{+0.15}_{-0.13}\) s in **15–5000 keV**, consistent with Fermi/GBM’s approximately 0.4 s in 50–300 keV. Using the ECLAIRs position of RA 19.059°, Dec +41.392° (J2000, 12.34′ radius at 90% confidence), GRM fitted the interval from −0.19 to +0.06 s relative to its trigger. The cutoff-power-law fit gave \(E_{\rm peak}=199^{+121}_{-48}\) keV and a 10–1000 keV fluence of \(1.98^{+0.47}_{-0.32}\times10^{-7}\) erg cm⁻². GBM’s published spectral fit used a shorter 0.14 s interval. [SVOM/GRM](https://gcn.nasa.gov/circulars/46383) · [ECLAIRs position](https://gcn.nasa.gov/circulars/46349) · [Fermi/GBM](https://gcn.nasa.gov/circulars/46360)

### Science News

#### ATLAS finds an excess of nearly spherical collisions in its first LHC search for QCD instantons

On **27 November 2026**, ATLAS presented a **3.5σ excess** in a search for transitions between topologically distinct configurations of the strong-interaction vacuum. The analysis used **1.6 nb⁻¹** of 13 TeV proton–proton data recorded in **2015**, with very few overlapping collisions. An instanton could produce many relatively soft particles spread in all directions. ATLAS used charged-track directions to test that pattern, estimating the background from control data and reweighted simulations of ordinary collisions. The preprint was submitted on **17 November**. [ATLAS briefing](https://atlas.cern/Updates/Briefing/Vacuum-Tunnelling) · [Analysis and appendices](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.22092v1) · [Collaboration figures](https://atlas.web.cern.ch/Atlas/GROUPS/PHYSICS/PAPERS/STDM-2021-01/)

In the most sensitive region, ATLAS counted **32,789 events**. The fit assigned about **28,800** to background and **3,900** to a possible signal. The observed significance is relative to the fitted background model. Uncertainty in modelling soft collisions dominates the signal-strength error, and theoretical instanton rates span orders of magnitude. The shape excess leaves open whether instantons caused it. [Results and background checks](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.22092v1)

##### History

The H1 collaboration reported a search for QCD instantons on **7 July 2016** using electron–proton collisions at HERA. It found no evidence and set cross-section limits in deep-inelastic scattering. ATLAS probes a different initial state and energy regime. H1’s limits therefore cannot be applied directly to the LHC excess. [H1 paper](https://doi.org/10.1140/epjc/s10052-016-4194-6)

#### BepiColombo’s longer Mercury orbit leaves less time for course corrections

At a London briefing on **27 November**, ESA reported that BepiColombo’s higher-than-planned orbit leaves less margin for manoeuvres before its two spacecraft separate. The insertion burn began on **21 November at 18:24 UTC**. An onboard software timing problem stopped it after about **28 of the planned 37 minutes**. Tracking confirmed a stable Mercury orbit with a period of about **75 hours**, compared with the planned **58 hours**. [ESA operations report, 23 November](https://www.esa.int/articles/about-us/directorates/operations/bepicolombo-safely-in-mercury-orbit-despite-unexpected-challenge) · [27 November briefing coverage and interview with mission operations](https://localnews8.com/social-feeds/2026/11/27/software-glitch-disrupts-spacecrafts-long-awaited-arrival-at-mercury/)

The longer period changes when the team can fire the thrusters and how long some burns must last. The two orbiters remain attached and healthy. Separation was still targeted for **9 December in Europe, 10 December in Japan**. ESA’s Mercury Planetary Orbiter will study the planet, while JAXA’s Mio will measure its magnetic environment from a different orbit. [JAXA status report, 26 November](https://www.isas.jaxa.jp/en/topics/004702.html) · [ESA mission timeline](https://www.esa.int/media/bepicolombo-arrival-at-mercury-timeline)

#### Two mouse genes encode the same 25-amino-acid protein but send it to different places

Fabiola Valdivia-Francia and colleagues screened short protein-coding sequences in mouse skin and followed up a gene called *Gm10076*. It encodes exactly the same 25-amino-acid sequence as RPL41, which sits between the two halves of the mature ribosome. Yet cryo-electron microscopy in knockout cells showed that the *Gm10076* copy could not fill the usual RPL41 site. When expressed in the context of its native RNA, the tagged protein accumulated in the nucleolus, where cells assemble ribosomes. Removing *Gm10076* disrupted the production of ribosome-assembly factors and reduced overall protein synthesis.

The results suggest that the RNA carrying a protein’s instructions can influence where that protein works, even when its amino-acid sequence is identical. The targeting mechanism remains open, and the authors could not exclude a contribution from an unannotated noncoding RNA. *Nature Cell Biology* received the paper on **23 July 2025**, accepted it on **12 October 2026** and published it on **27 November 2026**. [Paper, especially Figures 5–7](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41556-026-02131-3)

#### An electrolyser runs for 1,000 hours on a route from captured carbon dioxide to carbon monoxide

Hengzhou Liu and colleagues traced a durability problem in electrolysers fed with carbon-capture liquid to the flow of protons across a membrane. Too much proton flow damages the catalyst. Too little leaves too little CO₂ available at the reaction surface. Oxidising hydrogen gas at the anode kept the proton flow matched to the electrical current. A bicarbonate-fed cell operated for **1,000 hours** at about **1 V** and **100 mA cm⁻²**, with **over 80%** of the current going toward carbon monoxide production. [Author-hosted full paper](https://light.utoronto.ca/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/s41560-026-02158-8.pdf)

The team separately tested conversion of capture liquid made with air. Its process estimate of **22 GJ per tonne of CO** includes the energy needed to supply hydrogen and assumes **75%** efficiency for the water electrolyser producing it. The 1,000-hour result measures cell endurance. The tonne-scale figure is an engineering calculation whose value depends on those system assumptions. *Nature Energy* received the paper on **11 March**, accepted it on **29 September** and published it on **27 November 2026**. [Article and supplementary energy balance](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41560-026-02158-8)

#### Earthquake waves reveal a possible plume rising from the base of the Pacific mantle

Huacong Wang, Yumei He and Mingming Li compared shear-wave records from **three earthquakes** with waveforms calculated for different shapes of the deep mantle. Near the southeastern edge of the Pacific’s large low-velocity province, the data favour a structure in which shear waves travel **2–3% more slowly** than in the reference mantle. It reaches about **700 km above the core–mantle boundary**, with a southward-projecting head roughly two to three times as wide as its stem. [Study and extended-data model comparisons](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41561-026-02151-6) · [Supplementary waveform tests](https://static-content.springer.com/esm/art%3A10.1038%2Fs41561-026-02151-6/MediaObjects/41561_2026_2151_MOESM1_ESM.pdf)

The shape is consistent with a rising plume bent by lateral flow. Deformation of the province’s edge could produce similar waveforms. The observations constrain the geometry, while growth and motion are interpretations from geodynamic modelling. *Nature Geoscience* received the paper on **1 February**, accepted it on **21 October** and published it on **27 November 2026**. The publisher’s abstract, extended data and supplementary tests were available for review. [Geodynamic model input](https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.32967842)

#### Breathing distinguishes defensive freezing from recovery in mice

Baptiste Mahéo, Sophie Bagur and colleagues recorded respiration and hippocampal activity in **male mice** that could move between a threatening and a safe location. After reaching safety, motionless mice sometimes breathed at **2–4 Hz** while brief hippocampal bursts called sharp-wave ripples reactivated patterns associated with the experience. Mice that spent more time in this state relative to defensive freezing had lower subsequent stress scores, based on sleep, heart rate and time spent near the enclosure walls.

Disrupting the ripples as they occurred reduced slow-breathing recovery and increased later stress scores relative to delayed stimulation. Diazepam increased slow-breathing immobility even while suppressing the ripples, suggesting that the same outward behaviour can arise through different mechanisms. Stillness alone can therefore obscure the distinction between fear and recovery in mouse experiments. The study did not test recovery in humans. *Nature Neuroscience* received it on **18 July 2025**, accepted it on **14 October 2026** and published it on **27 November 2026**. [Research article](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-026-02519-2) · [Authors’ data record](https://zenodo.org/records/22753351)
