### Verified science news

#### ★★★ Two independent teams operate the first thorium-229 nuclear clocks

**Peer-reviewed results published 7 October 2026 in _Nature_.**

Two independent collaborations have crossed the boundary from spectroscopy of the unusually low-energy \(^{229}\mathrm{Th}\) isomer to an operating clock: in each experiment, a continuous-wave 148-nm laser is actively steered by the nuclear transition itself. Both use \(^{229}\mathrm{Th}\) embedded in calcium-fluoride crystals at room temperature, but employ different vacuum-ultraviolet sources and readout implementations. _Nature_ describes them as the first implementations of a nuclear clock.

Luca Toscani De Col and collaborators report a shot-noise-limited fractional-frequency instability of

\[
3\times10^{-12}/\sqrt{\tau/{\rm s}},
\]

approaching \(10^{-15}\) after one day. A subharmonic of the clock laser is continuously compared with a \(^{171}\mathrm{Yb}^{+}\) single-ion clock. Searching for periodic or drifting changes over timescales from approximately 20 seconds to one day also produces competitive ultralight-dark-matter limits, including improved sensitivity to coupling through the strong interaction. ([Nature paper](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11084-4); [open arXiv version](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.04997))

Beichen Huang and collaborators obtain

\[
5\times10^{-13}/\sqrt{\tau/{\rm s}},
\]

again averaging into the \(10^{-15}\) range. Importantly for reproducibility, clocks made from two independently fabricated crystals agree in transition frequency at the \(10^{-13}\) level. ([Nature paper](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11122-1); [Tsinghua announcement](https://www.phys.tsinghua.edu.cn/phyen/info/1154/2344.htm))

##### History

The \(^{229}\mathrm{Th}\) isomer has been pursued as a nuclear-clock reference since [Peik and Tamm’s 2003 proposal](https://doi.org/10.1209/epl/i2003-00210-x). Radiative decay was detected in a crystal in [2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-05894-z), followed by direct laser excitation and an optical-frequency comparison with a strontium clock in [2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07839-6), and continuous-wave absorption spectroscopy in [September 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11011-7). Those experiments located and characterized the transition; the new papers close the feedback loop, making the nucleus the discriminator that controls the oscillator.

The devices do **not** yet outperform the best electronic optical clocks, whose uncertainties approach \(10^{-19}\). Crystal-dependent frequency shifts, spatial inhomogeneity, temperature and Zeeman control, vacuum-ultraviolet power, and absolute systematic evaluation remain limiting. The immediate achievement is not a timekeeping record but the independent demonstration of a reproducible nuclear reference with unusual sensitivity to variations of fundamental constants and ultralight dark matter.

#### EP261010a and GRB 261009A: transient follow-up

**Status through 10 October 2026, GCN Circular 45882.**

[EP261010a](https://gcn.nasa.gov/circulars/45880) is a newly detected Einstein Probe soft-X-ray transient, triggered at **00:18:44 UTC on 10 October**, with an uncatalogued FXT counterpart. [Nordic Optical Telescope imaging](https://gcn.nasa.gov/circulars/45882) found no optical source to \(r>23.9\) and \(z>21.5\) approximately 1.4 hours later. Its duration, spectrum, distance, and physical class were unreported at the 10 October cutoff; the source remained unclassified.

[GRB 261009A](https://gcn.nasa.gov/circulars/45879) was reported as a likely short Fermi-GBM burst with a \(3.2^\circ\) statistical localization. No secure counterpart or multimessenger association has been announced.

### Science Radar

#### ★★★ Optogenetic therapy moves from one patient to a 10-person cohort

**Peer-reviewed original article published online 7 October and in the 8 October 2026 issue of the _New England Journal of Medicine_.**

José-Alain Sahel, Stefan Futterknecht, Isabelle Audo, Joseph N. Martel, Shouyu Ling, Chloé Pagot, Angelo Arleo, and colleagues report the first clinical cohort treated with ganglion-cell-directed optogenetic therapy for advanced retinitis pigmentosa.

Each of 10 blind or nearly blind participants received an intravitreal adeno-associated-virus vector encoding the red-shifted channelrhodopsin ChrimsonR in the worse-seeing eye. Custom goggles converted scenes into light patterns designed to stimulate the newly photosensitive retinal ganglion cells. ([Primary paper](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2602215); [institutional summary](https://iob.ch/news-insights/optogenetic-therapy-shows-safe-step-forward-in-vision-restoration/))

The primary outcome was safety. The study recorded 34 ocular adverse events among nine participants: 23 mild, 10 moderate, and one severe transient central-retinal-artery occlusion that resolved within minutes. No systemic treatment-related adverse event was reported.

Light sensitivity increased in seven of 10 participants by factors ranging from 2.0 to 62.3; six crossed the authors’ threshold for a clinically meaningful, at least fourfold improvement. Several participants could locate simple objects, find doorways, or follow lines while wearing the goggles, and visually evoked brain signals were measurable.

##### History

In [2021](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-021-01351-4), the same program reported partial object perception in a single treated patient—the first clinical demonstration that optogenetic stimulation could restore any visual function in a blind person. The new study advances the evidence from an individual proof of concept to a reproducible early cohort, with follow-up extending to roughly five years.

The method is potentially mutation-independent: instead of replacing one defective gene in dying photoreceptors, it makes surviving downstream retinal neurons light-sensitive. That could broaden applicability across genetically heterogeneous retinal degeneration.

This remains an open-label, uncontrolled Phase I/II safety study involving only 10 people. The clinically meaningful light-sensitivity threshold was literature-based but not prespecified. Benefits required specialized goggles and training, varied across participants, and did not extend to reading or face recognition. Viable ganglion cells and an intact optic nerve are also necessary. Larger controlled trials must establish efficacy, durability, dose dependence, and real-world functional benefit.
